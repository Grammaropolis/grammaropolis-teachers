# Item design

How to choose item types, write clean items, and score the result. The correctness gate in the skill's Step 5 is the final check; this file is how to write items that pass it the first time.

## Shape of a check

| | Exit ticket | Quiz |
|---|---|---|
| Items | 3-5 (default 4; default 3 at grades 1-2) | 6-10 |
| Time | about 5 minutes (grades 1-2: 3-4 minutes, read aloud) | 10-15 minutes |
| Standards | usually one, occasionally two closely related | up to three or four, grouped so items on the same standard sit together |
| Production item | at least one apply item; a write-your-own item when there's room | at least one write-your-own item |

**The climb.** Order items from recognizing, to telling the concept apart from its nearest look-alike, to fixing or using it, to producing it. A four-item ticket might run: identify, discriminate, fix, write. The discrimination item is usually the most diagnostic one on the page, because it's where the misconception shows.

## Item types

| Type | Best for | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Multiple choice | recognizing and discriminating | Every distractor is a named misconception. Grades 1-2: three options, except a subject-verb agreement fill-in, which may have two (*walk*, *walks*) rather than a filler third. Grade 3: four options, or three when a fourth would only be filler. Grades 4-8: four options. |
| Identify (students circle or underline) | parts of speech, subjects and predicates, clauses, phrases | The target sentence has exactly one correct answer; say "the" (one) or "each" (several) and match the key to it. |
| Sort or match | kinds of nouns, sentence types, synonym and antonym pairs | Keep to 4-6 elements; each fits exactly one bucket. |
| Fix the sentence | punctuation, capitalization, agreement, fragments and run-ons | Tell the student how many errors there are. The key lists every acceptable fix. |
| Choose the word | frequently confused words, pronoun case, verb forms, conjunctions by meaning | A sentence with a blank and 2-4 choices. Only one choice is standard in that slot. |
| Write your own | using the concept | Give a clear frame ("Write one sentence that..."). The key gives acceptance criteria, a model, and the likely near-miss. |
| Explain why (grades 5-8) | justifying a choice | One sentence. The key lists the idea a complete answer must include. |
| Combine or revise (grades 3-8) | sentence combining, concision, variety | The key accepts any version that meets the stated criteria and lists two or three. |

## Grade bands

**Grades 1-2.** Default to a read-aloud version: the teacher reads each item; students circle, underline, or write one word or one short sentence. Three items, occasionally four. Target sentences of about 4-8 words using common, decodable words. Three options per multiple-choice item (two for a subject-verb agreement fill-in, where a third would only be filler). No "which is NOT" items. A write-your-own item may offer a sentence starter. Include a short read-aloud script in the key.

**Grades 3-5.** Four or five items for an exit ticket. Target sentences up to about 12-15 words. Four options (grade 3 may use three when a fourth would be filler; a filler option no student would choose tells the teacher nothing). Light use of real grammatical terms in stems ("Which word is the coordinating conjunction?"). One write-your-own or fix item per ticket.

**Grades 6-8.** Items can ask for function and justification, not only identification ("What job does the phrase in quotation marks do?"). An explain-why item fits well. Stems can be longer, but the difficulty lives in the grammar, never in the reading load.

## Writing the stem

- **One clear task per item.** Put the whole question in the stem, so a student could answer it without reading the options.
- **Positive stems.** Avoid "Which is NOT..." items; if one is truly needed (grades 5-8 only), write NOT in capitals.
- **Mark targets in a way that survives plain text.** The student page gets pasted, printed, and projected, so underlining, bold, and italics may vanish. Quote the target in the stem ("What job does the word 'quickly' do?"), write it in capitals and say so ("the word in capitals"), or name its position ("the second word"). Never write "the underlined word."
- **Neutral, original content.** School, home, nature, sports, food, animals, and made-up towns. Invented names from a range of backgrounds. No real people, brands, companies, or products (for capitalizing product names, invent one). Nothing frightening, sad, or controversial. No famous quotations.
- **Don't reuse the lesson's examples.** New sentences check transfer.

## Writing the options

- **Exactly one correct answer, and it's unambiguously correct** in standard written American English.
- **Every distractor is a specific misconception** from `reference_misconceptions.md` or one you can name as precisely. Record it: the misconception becomes a line in the key's grouping guide, and the rule the option breaks becomes part of the item's Why not line.
- **Parallel options.** Similar length, same grammatical form, same level of detail. When options are sentences, change only the element being tested so the student is judging the rule, not spotting the odd one out.
- **No "all of the above," "none of the above,"** "both A and B," or joke options.
- **Balance answer positions.** Across a check, spread correct answers over A, B, C, and D (A, B, and C when items have three options); never more than two in a row in the same position; not every correct answer in the middle.

## Items that need special handling

- **Hyphens and dashes (grades 6-8).** This skill's output never contains a dash glyph, so dash items can't print one. Have students insert the mark ("Add a dash where the sentence breaks off") or choose where it goes, with options that name positions in words ("after *finally*"). The key describes the correct position in words. Hyphen items are fine as printed, since the hyphen is an ordinary character.
- **Serial comma.** Grammaropolis uses the Oxford comma in everything it writes, including items. Never make the serial comma itself the thing being tested, since style guides differ; for series items, test the commas every style requires.
- **Title capitalization.** Test only what most classroom styles agree on: capitalize the first word and every noun, verb, adjective, and adverb; keep *a*, *an*, *the*, *and*, *but*, *or*, and short prepositions such as *of*, *in*, *on*, and *at* lowercase in the middle. Short verbs like *is* and *are* are verbs, so they're capitalized. Most classroom styles also capitalize the last word, but not all do (APA, for one, lowercases a short preposition or conjunction even at the end), so never build an item on a title that ends in a short word of that kind. Never key a longer preposition (*about*, *with*, *from*), the short conjunctions *so*, *yet*, *for*, and *nor*, the word *as*, or the *to* in an infinitive ("How to Build a Kite") either way, because styles differ on them. If a teacher says the class follows a particular style, follow it.
- **Writing checks.** Test function, not taste: "Which sentence states the writer's opinion?" is fair; "Which is the best hook?" is not.
- **Vocabulary.** Synonyms are rarely exact. Ask for the word "closest in meaning," not "means the same as."

## Diagnosis first, tiers optional

The key leads with what the teacher acts on: which wrong answers point to which misconception, and what to reteach (the grouping guide in the output format). Score each item as right or not yet (write-your-own items are right when they meet every acceptance criterion). A quiz that covers more than one standard also reports a subtotal per standard, because "7 of 10" hides which skill is shaky.

**Optional tiers.** Grammaropolis checks can also report in four tiers, as one line after the grouping guide. The tiers describe where a student is on this check, not a grade. One rule applies at every length:

- **Gold:** every item right.
- **Silver:** at least two-thirds right, but not all.
- **Bronze:** at least half right, but less than two-thirds.
- **Not Yet:** less than half right.

The table applies that rule. A 3-item ticket has no Bronze row, because no score falls between half and two-thirds.

| Items | Gold | Silver | Bronze | Not Yet |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 3 | 2 | (none) | 0-1 |
| 4 | 4 | 3 | 2 | 0-1 |
| 5 | 5 | 4 | 3 | 0-2 |
| 6 | 6 | 4-5 | 3 | 0-2 |
| 7 | 7 | 5-6 | 4 | 0-3 |
| 8 | 8 | 6-7 | 4-5 | 0-3 |
| 9 | 9 | 6-8 | 5 | 0-4 |
| 10 | 10 | 7-9 | 5-6 | 0-4 |

What each tier suggests for tomorrow:

- **Gold:** ready to move on or to use the concept in writing.
- **Silver:** solid; a quick look at the item or items missed is usually enough.
- **Bronze:** getting there; a short small-group reteach aimed at the misconception the wrong answers point to.
- **Not Yet:** needs another pass through the concept before more practice. More practice on a misunderstanding just practices the misunderstanding.
