# Output format

Every check is delivered as one chat message in two separate blocks: a **student page** in its own copy-ready block that the teacher can print, project, or paste as is, then a **teacher key** in a second block below a clear divider. The student page never shows an answer, a standard code, or a misconception note, and nothing from the key is mixed into it.

## The student page

- **Plain text that survives copying.** No bold, italics, or underlining, since they vanish when pasted into a document or a form. Mark a target word by quoting it or by writing it in capitals and saying so ("the word in capitals").
- **Title as a plain line** (no "#" or other markup, which would print literally), then **header lines:** Name, Period, and Date, each with a blank. Leave off Period at grades 1-2 unless the teacher wants it.
- **Answer lines** under every fix-the-sentence, write-your-own, and explain-why item: one line for a fix, two lines for a sentence the student writes.
- **Right after the block**, outside it, one short line telling the teacher a half-sheet version (two copies to a page) is available on request.

## The teacher key

In this order:

1. **Standards** line (with the teacher's own code too, if they gave one) and **Time**.
2. **Answers at a glance:** one line, for example "1-C, 2-B, 3 see key, 4 see key." This is what the teacher grades from.
3. **Subtotals** (quizzes that cover more than one standard only): which items assess which standard and how many points each standard is worth, so the teacher can score each skill separately.
4. **Grouping guide:** one line per diagnostic wrong answer, or per group of wrong answers that reveal the same idea: what the student chose, the misconception it points to, and one concrete reteach move. This replaces a separate "If they chose..." line under every item. Keep it to the answers that actually diagnose something; a 10-item quiz usually needs six to ten lines here, not one per distractor.
5. **Tiers (optional):** one line from the tier table for this many items.
6. **Item detail:** for each item, the answer, a one-line rule, a **Why not** line naming the rule each wrong option breaks, and the standard. Fix-the-sentence items list every acceptable fix; write-your-own items give criteria, a model, and the likely near-miss.
7. **Reading the results:** two or three lines on the patterns that matter most, then, optionally, the one light pointer.

For a quiz, the title reads "QUIZ" instead of "EXIT TICKET," and items on the same standard sit together under a short section label on the student page.

For grades 1-2, add a **Read-aloud script** right after the key's Answers at a glance line: exactly what the teacher says for each item ("Number one. Listen: The frog hopped onto the log. Circle the word that names an animal."), so the page itself can stay nearly word-free.

## On request

Offer these in the closing line; build them only when asked.

- **Version A and Version B** (quizzes and retakes): same standards, same item types, and the same misconceptions in the distractors, with new sentences and shuffled answer positions. Each version gets its own Answers at a glance line.
- **A plain question-and-answer list** for pasting into a digital form: each item's stem, its options, and the correct answer on its own line, with no formatting.
- **A half-sheet layout:** the student page sized to print two to a page.
- **A read-aloud version** for any grade.

## Worked example: Grade 4, commas in compound sentences

Chosen because it's a single, clean standard with a sharp look-alike (a compound predicate, which takes no comma). Every compound sentence below has five or more words in each clause, so the comma is required, not optional.

```
EXIT TICKET: Commas in Compound Sentences (Grade 4)

Name: ____________________   Period: ______   Date: ____________

Chief Comma here! A comma goes before a FANBOYS word only when that word joins two complete sentences. Find the sentences that follow the rule.

1. Which sentence uses commas correctly?
   A. Our class, planted six tomato seeds and the first sprouts appeared on Friday.
   B. Our class planted six tomato seeds and, the first sprouts appeared on Friday.
   C. Our class planted six tomato seeds, and the first sprouts appeared on Friday.
   D. Our class planted six tomato seeds and the first sprouts, appeared on Friday.

2. Which sentence needs a comma before the word "and"?
   A. Marisol fed the hungry cat and then filled its water bowl.
   B. Marisol fed the hungry cat and her brother watered the plants.
   C. Marisol and her brother fed the hungry cat after school.
   D. Marisol fed the hungry cat and the goldfish after school.

3. This sentence is missing one comma. Write the sentence with the comma added.
   Quentin practiced his lines all week but he still felt nervous before the show.
   ______________________________________________________

4. Write one compound sentence about recess. Join two complete sentences with a comma and the word "so."
   ______________________________________________________
   ______________________________________________________
```

(Half-sheet version, two to a page, available on request.)

```
TEACHER KEY: Commas in Compound Sentences (Grade 4)

Standards: L.4.2.C (use a comma before a coordinating conjunction in a compound sentence)
Time: about 5 minutes
Answers at a glance: 1-C, 2-B, 3 see key, 4 see key

GROUPING GUIDE
- Chose 2A: reads a second verb after "and" as the start of a new sentence (a compound predicate). Reteach: cover everything before "and"; "then filled its water bowl" has no subject, so it can't stand alone. This is the most useful signal on the page.
- Chose 2C or 2D: puts a comma before every "and" (a compound subject or object). Reteach: check both sides for a subject and a verb before adding the comma.
- Chose 1B, or put the comma after "but" on 3: attaches the comma to the conjunction. Reteach: the comma and the FANBOYS word travel together, comma first.
- Chose 1A or 1D, or put the comma somewhere else on 3: places commas where they'd pause, including between a subject and its verb. Reteach: commas follow rules, not breaths; never separate a subject from its verb.
- Right on 1-3 but no comma on 4: recognition is there and production isn't yet. Reteach: a two-minute warm-up tomorrow where each student writes one compound sentence.

Tiers (optional): 4 correct: Gold. 3: Silver. 2: Bronze. 0-1: Not Yet.

ITEM DETAIL
1. Answer: C
   Rule: In a compound sentence, the comma goes right before the coordinating conjunction.
   Why not: A separates the subject "Our class" from its verb and leaves out the comma before "and"; B puts the comma after the conjunction instead of before it; D separates the subject "the first sprouts" from its verb and leaves out the comma before "and."
   Standard: L.4.2.C

2. Answer: B
   Rule: Only a sentence that joins two complete sentences needs the comma; "her brother watered the plants" can stand alone.
   Why not: A joins two verbs that share one subject, so no comma is needed; C joins two subjects; D joins two objects of "fed." None of them has a complete sentence after "and."
   Standard: L.4.2.C

3. Answer: Quentin practiced his lines all week, but he still felt nervous before the show.
   Rule: "But" joins two complete sentences here, so a comma goes before it.
   Accept: only a comma directly before "but."
   Standard: L.4.2.C

4. Answer: varies
   Accept any sentence that: has a complete sentence on each side of "so" (each with its own subject and verb), has a comma directly before "so," and uses "so" to show a result.
   Model: The kickball rolled onto the school roof, so we started a game of tag instead.
   Near-miss: "The kickball rolled onto the school roof, so started a game of tag." The second part has no subject, so it isn't a complete sentence; this student may be the one who chose 2A.
   Near-miss: "The kickball rolled onto the school roof so we started a game of tag instead." Two complete sentences joined by "so," but no comma; the rule hasn't transferred to their own writing yet.
   Standard: L.4.2.C

READING THE RESULTS
- A cluster on 2A matters most: those students add a comma before any "and" followed by a verb. Reteach with three sentence pairs, covering the first half of each and asking, "Can this stand alone?"
- Scattered errors on 1 (A, B, and D) point back to the rule itself; reteach it before more practice.

If your class wants more practice with commas, the Punctuation Department at grammaropolis.com/learn/pd picks up from here.
```

## Rules the example shows

- The student page is its own block, with Name, Period, and Date lines, answer lines under the fix and write items, and no bold, italics, underlining, checkmarks, or hints. Target words are quoted.
- Options within an item are close in length and form. In item 1 only the comma moves, so students judge the rule, not the wording.
- Correct answers sit in different positions (C, then B).
- The key opens with Answers at a glance and a grouping guide; the item detail carries a Why not line that names the rule each wrong option breaks, so anyone can check the key.
- The fix-the-sentence item says how many errors there are, and the key lists what to accept.
- The write-your-own item has criteria, a model, and near-misses tied back to earlier items.
- The framing line is in the character's own voice.
- Oxford comma throughout, in both the page and the key; zero em dashes and zero en dashes.
- One pointer at most, at the very end, framed as more practice, never as a pitch.
