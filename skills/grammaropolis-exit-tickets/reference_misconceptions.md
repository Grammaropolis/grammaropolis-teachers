# Misconceptions and hazards

Two lists per topic.

- **Misconceptions** are the wrong ideas students actually hold. Build distractors from them, so each wrong answer tells the teacher which idea to reteach. Each comes with a one-line reteach move the key can borrow.
- **Hazards** are where item writers accidentally create a second correct answer. Every item on the topic is checked against its hazards before delivery. A hazard marked **never build an item on this** means exactly that: no stem, no option, and no fix-the-sentence item may turn on it. A hazard marked **only in this pinned form** may be tested, but only in the form given.

## Usage myths and style choices: never key these as errors, in any topic

Style guides and usage experts don't treat these as errors, so an item that marks them wrong has a defensible second answer.

- Ending a sentence with a preposition ("Who did you go with?")
- Starting a sentence with *and*, *but*, *or*, or *because*
- Splitting an infinitive ("to quickly finish")
- Singular *they* with an indefinite or unknown antecedent ("Everyone grabbed their coat.")
- The serial comma, present or absent
- *Who* versus *whom* in casual speech, and *that* versus *who* for people
- *Less* versus *fewer*. **Never build an item on this**, in any context: usage guides disagree about where the line falls ("less than ten minutes," "one less thing"), so a keyed answer can always be argued.
- "I feel good" (a linking verb plus an adjective, which is standard)
- A comma after a short introductory element, whether a phrase or a clause ("In June we moved." and "When it snowed we stayed in." are as correct as the versions with commas)
- A comma between two short independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction ("I ran and she walked." is correct with or without the comma)

**The five-word rule for optional commas.** Key a comma after an introductory element only when that element is five or more words long ("After the long drive to the lake, we..."). Key a comma before the conjunction in a compound sentence only when each independent clause is five or more words long. Five words is a Grammaropolis house threshold, set on the conservative side on purpose: style guides say only that a comma may be left out when the element or the clauses are short, without naming a length, so anything shorter could have two answers.

## Parts of Speech

**Nouns**
- Misconceptions: nouns are only things you can touch (misses *courage*, *idea*, *friendship*); any capitalized word is a proper noun (including the first word of a sentence); a specific-sounding common noun is proper ("my teacher"); an apostrophe makes a noun plural ("apple's for sale"); irregular plurals follow the -s rule (*childs*, *mouses*); a collective noun names only one person or thing (misses that *flock* or *team* names a group). Reteach: "If you can put *the* or *a* in front of it and it names a person, place, thing, or idea, it's a noun, even if you can't touch it."
- Hazards: words that are nouns or verbs by context (*run*, *play*, *paint*, *walk*), so the sentence must pin the role; nouns used as modifiers (*school* in "school bus"), which some classrooms call adjectives, so never make one the keyed noun or a distractor; *-ing* words working as nouns (gerunds), which younger students are taught are verbs.

**Action verbs and verb tense**
- Misconceptions: only physical actions are verbs (misses *think*, *want*, *know*); the helping verb isn't part of the verb ("has **finished**" instead of "**has finished**"); the *to* form or an *-ing* form is the main verb; irregular past tense takes -ed (*runned*, *teached*, *buyed*); tense can shift mid-sentence without a reason. Reteach: "Find the word that changes when you move the sentence to yesterday: that's your verb."
- Hazards: sensory verbs (*look*, *feel*, *taste*, *smell*, *sound*, *grow*, *turn*) can be action or linking verbs; *have* and *do* can be main or helping verbs. **An *-ing* word after *is* is not automatically part of the verb.** After a helping *is* it usually is ("Marisol **is painting** the fence," the progressive). After a linking *is* it may be a gerund working as a noun ("Her hobby is **painting**") or an adjective ("The movie is **boring**"). **Only in this pinned form:** the stem must settle the role, for example with a direct object after the *-ing* word ("is painting the fence") for a verb phrase, or by asking about a sentence where no other reading is possible.

**Verb mood (grade 8)**
- Misconceptions: any sentence with *if* is subjunctive; a command has no subject at all (it has an understood *you*); every command ends with an exclamation point; a sentence can switch moods midstream without a problem ("Clean your desk, and then you should stack the books." starts as a command and turns into a statement). Reteach: "Ask what the sentence is doing: stating a fact, giving a command, asking a question, saying what would happen, or imagining something that isn't true."
- Hazards: **Only in these pinned forms:** a classify-the-mood item names the mood of a clear case: a contrary-to-fact *were* clause ("If I were a giraffe, I could reach the top shelf," where the item asks about the *were* clause only and the answer is subjunctive), an imperative ("Hand me the glue."), a question ("Did the paint dry?"), or a plain statement of fact ("The paint dried."). **Conditional is never an option on an item about a *were* clause**, because the whole sentence is also a textbook conditional sentence, so "conditional" would be defensible. Conditional mood is tested only through a production item or not at all: "Write one sentence that tells what would happen if it snowed all day." Accept any sentence with an *if* clause and a clause using *would*, *could*, or *might* that tells the result; accept *was* or *were* in the *if* clause. A mood-shift item asks students to fix the shift, and the key accepts only the version that makes every clause a command (for the example above, "Clean your desk, and then stack the books."); it never asks students to name the mood of a clause that contains *should*, *would*, or *could*. **Never build an item on** a fill-in that asks students to choose *was* or *were* after *if* ("If Kenji ___ taller, he could reach the hoop"), since *was* is common in standard informal writing; *were* appears only in classify-the-mood items, where the task is naming the mood, not choosing the verb. Also never build an item on the mood of a real conditional ("If it rains, we'll stay inside."), which sources label differently; a mandative subjunctive ("I suggest that he go" versus "goes"), where both forms appear in edited English; or "I wish I was" versus "I wish I were."

**Linking verbs**
- Misconceptions: *is* isn't a verb because nothing happens; every *is* is a linking verb (misses the helping *is* in "is running"); sensory verbs are always action verbs. Reteach: "Swap in *is* or *seems*. If the sentence still makes sense and the word after the verb describes or renames the subject, it's linking."
- Hazards: "The baker smelled the fresh loaves" (action) versus "The fresh loaves smelled sweet" (linking); "Rosalind turned the key" versus "The maple leaves turned orange." The stem must allow only one reading.

**Adjectives**
- Misconceptions: adjectives only come before nouns (misses "The sky is **gray**"); adjectives and adverbs are interchangeable (*real good*, *drove careful*); every -er word is a comparative adjective; double comparatives are fine (*more taller*); proper adjectives stay lowercase (*mexican food*). Reteach: "Adjectives answer which one, what kind, or how many about a noun, and after a linking verb they still describe the subject."
- Hazards: whether articles (*a*, *an*, *the*) and possessives (*my*, *her*) count as adjectives varies by classroom, so never ask students to count adjectives; nouns used as modifiers; participles (*broken* window) below grade 8.

**Adverbs**
- Misconceptions: every -ly word is an adverb (misses *friendly*, *lovely*, *lonely*, which are adjectives); every adverb ends in -ly (misses *fast*, *soon*, *very*, *not*, *always*); adverbs only modify verbs (misses *very* tall, *too* quickly); *good* and *well* are interchangeable after action verbs. Reteach: "Adverbs answer how, when, where, or how much, and they can describe verbs, adjectives, and other adverbs."
- Hazards: *fast*, *hard*, *late*, *early*, *daily* are adjectives or adverbs by position; *well* is also an adjective meaning healthy; *up*, *down*, *in*, *out*, *inside* are adverbs with no object and prepositions with one; verb-plus-particle phrases ("look **up** the word") are classified differently by different sources, so avoid them.

**Pronouns**
- Misconceptions: *me* works as a subject in a pair ("Me and Sam went"); *I* sounds more correct everywhere ("between you and I"); *myself* can replace *I* or *me* ("Sam and myself"); *it's* is possessive; a pronoun can point to either of two nouns without confusion. Reteach for a subject pair: "Cover the other person. Would you say 'me went'? Then it's *I*." Reteach for an object pair: "Which word comes right before the pair? After a preposition (*between*, *with*, *for*, *to*) or after an action verb, use *me*, *him*, *her*, *us*, or *them*: 'between you and **me**.'"
- Hazards: see the usage myths (singular *they*, *who* and *whom*); *this*, *that*, *these*, and *those* are demonstrative pronouns when they stand alone and demonstrative adjectives when they sit before a noun; *his* and *her* before a noun are taught differently in different curricula, so don't ask students to classify them. **Never build an item on:** a pronoun after *than* or *as* ("taller than me" versus "taller than I"), since both appear in edited English; a pronoun after a linking verb ("It is I" versus "It is me"); or a pronoun before a gerund ("him helping" versus "his helping"). Test case with compound subjects, compound objects, and objects of prepositions instead.

**Relative pronouns and relative adverbs (grade 4 and up)**
- Misconceptions: *which* works for people ("the coach which cheered"); *who* works for things ("the kite who flew"); *who's* shows ownership instead of *whose*; any *that* is a relative pronoun. Reteach: "Match the word to what it points back to: *who* for people, *which* for things, *whose* for an owner, *where* for a place, *when* for a time."
- Hazards: **Never build an item on** *that* versus *which* in a clause that limits the noun ("the bike that I fixed" and "the bike which I fixed" are both standard); *who* versus *that* for people (a usage myth); *the day that* versus *the day when*, or *the reason that* versus *the reason why*, where both are standard; *whose* for a thing ("a tree whose leaves fell"), which is standard; *who* versus *whom* as an object, even in writing ("the teacher ___ we thanked"); *who* versus *which* or *that* for an animal ("the puppy ___ wagged its tail"), since writers use both for pets. **Only in this pinned form:** a *who* versus *which* item contrasts a human with an inanimate object ("the pilot who landed the plane," "the kite which crashed"). **Only in this pinned form:** key *where* only after a real place noun ("the park where we skate"), and never mark *where* wrong after a word like *time*, *case*, or *situation*.

**Conjunctions**
- Misconceptions: a comma alone can join two sentences (comma splice); *but* fits any connection (misses that it signals contrast); a *because* clause can stand alone as a sentence; correlatives can mix (*either... nor*); every *and* joins two sentences. Reteach: "Each FANBOYS word tells how two ideas relate: *but* means contrast, *so* means result, *or* means choice."
- Hazards: *after*, *before*, *since*, *until*, *as* are subordinating conjunctions before a clause and prepositions before a noun ("after the game" versus "after the game ended"); *for* as a coordinating conjunction sounds old-fashioned, so don't key it at grades 1-4; *so* also works as an adverb ("so happy"); *yet* also works as an adverb ("not yet").

**Prepositions**
- Misconceptions: prepositions only show place (misses *during*, *after*, *about*, *with*, *of*); the noun right before the verb is the subject even when it's inside a prepositional phrase ("The box of crayons **are**"); *to* is always a preposition (misses the infinitive "to run"). Reteach: "A preposition starts a phrase that ends with a noun or pronoun; cross the phrase out and the subject and verb are easier to see."
- Hazards: a word with no object isn't a preposition ("She went **inside**"); ending with a preposition is not an error; the conjunction overlap above.

**Interjections**
- Misconceptions: interjections always take an exclamation point (misses the mild ones: "**Well,** I think so."); an interjection must come first. Reteach: "Strong feeling gets an exclamation point; mild feeling gets a comma."
- Hazards: *well*, *yes*, *no*, *oh* also have other jobs, so don't ask students to classify them in isolation; whether a strong or mild interjection is intended is often a judgment call, so give context that decides it.

## Punctuation Department

**End marks**
- Misconceptions: a sentence that mentions a question needs a question mark ("She asked where I was?"); every command needs an exclamation point; long sentences get exclamation points. Reteach: "Is the sentence itself asking? Then it gets a question mark. Reporting a question is a statement."
- Hazards: many sentences can take a period or an exclamation point depending on the speaker's feeling. Never key period versus exclamation point unless the context settles it ("she shouted"), and prefer questions versus statements, which are clear-cut.

**Commas**
- Misconceptions: a comma goes wherever you pause (commas between subject and verb: "The tall girl, ran"); a comma alone can join two sentences; a comma goes before every *and* (misses compound predicates: in "She ran and jumped," a comma before *and* is not needed); the comma goes after the conjunction ("and, then"). Reteach: "Commas follow rules, not breaths. Before a FANBOYS word, check whether both sides could be sentences on their own."
- Hazards: the serial comma; a comma after a short introductory phrase or clause, and a comma between two short independent clauses (apply the five-word rule above); a comma before *too* at the end of a sentence (optional); a comma before *and* in a compound predicate is "not needed," so a key may call it unnecessary but never say it breaks a rule; restrictive versus nonrestrictive elements depend on meaning, so the stem must settle it ("my brother Sam" is only correct without commas if the student has more than one brother); coordinate adjectives need a clear test case (use pairs where the *and* test is decisive, like "a cold, rainy day" versus "a small wooden box").

**Capitalization**
- Misconceptions: seasons and school subjects are capitalized (*Summer*, *Math*, though *English* and *Spanish* are); directions are always capitalized; family words are always capitalized, or never ("my mom" versus "Mom said"); every word in a title is capitalized. Reteach: "Capitals mark names: one particular person, place, or thing."
- Hazards: title rules for longer prepositions vary; *north* versus *the North* depends on meaning; the first word after a colon varies by style.

**Apostrophes**
- Misconceptions: an apostrophe makes a word plural; *it's* shows possession; the apostrophe goes where the letters touch, not where letters were removed (*do'nt*); the apostrophe position for plural possessives ("the girl's coats" for many girls). Reteach: "Possessive or contraction? Missing letters get an apostrophe right where they left; owners get 's, or just ' after a plural that already ends in s."
- Hazards: singular possessive versus plural possessive depends on how many owners, so the stem must say ("one dog" or "three dogs"); possessives of singular names ending in s (*James's* or *James'*) vary by style, so never key them.

**Quotation marks**
- Misconceptions: the period or comma goes outside the closing quotation mark (in US English it goes inside); reported speech gets quotation marks (putting them around *that she was tired* in "She said that she was tired."); the second half of a split quotation starts with a capital letter even when it continues the same sentence. Reteach: "Quotation marks hug the speaker's exact words, and in American English the comma or period stays inside the hug."
- Hazards: a question mark goes inside only when the quotation itself is a question; titles: short works in quotation marks, long works in italics or underlined, and a typed item may not show italics, so describe it in words if needed.

**Semicolons and colons**
- Misconceptions: a semicolon can join a sentence to a phrase or a dependent clause; a semicolon and a FANBOYS word together are needed ("; and"); a colon goes after a verb or preposition before a list ("My favorites are: ..."); a semicolon introduces a list. Reteach: "A semicolon needs a full sentence on both sides. A colon needs a full sentence before it."
- Hazards: a period and a semicolon are both correct between two related sentences, so never ask students to choose between them; capitalization after a colon varies by style; semicolons in a series whose items already contain commas are a grade 5 and up skill.

**Parentheses and brackets**
- Misconceptions: the sentence's period goes inside the parentheses when the parenthetical is only part of the sentence; brackets and parentheses are interchangeable. Reteach: "Parentheses hold the writer's own aside; brackets hold words the writer adds inside someone else's quotation."
- Hazards: commas, parentheses, and dashes can all set off the same nonrestrictive element, so never ask which of the three is correct unless the item names the effect wanted.

**Hyphens and dashes**
- Misconceptions: hyphens and dashes are the same mark; a hyphen can stand in for a dash; -ly adverbs take a hyphen ("a quickly-moving car" should be "a quickly moving car"). Reteach: "A hyphen joins words into one idea; a dash breaks a sentence to add emphasis or a sudden turn."
- Hazards: a compound modifier before a noun is hyphenated ("a well-known author"), but after the noun the rule varies by dictionary and style, so never key it; this skill never prints a dash glyph, so dash items ask students to insert the mark or describe its position in words.

## Sentence Factory

**Subjects and predicates**
- Misconceptions: the subject is always the first word or the first noun; the noun in a prepositional phrase is the subject; *there* or *here* is the subject in "There are..." sentences; a command has no subject (it's an understood *you*). Reteach: "Find the verb first, then ask who or what is doing it."
- Hazards: say whether you want the simple or the complete subject (or predicate) and key it that way; compound subjects and verbs need "circle **each**."

**Subject-verb agreement (Vinny)**
- Misconceptions: the verb agrees with the nearest noun ("The box of crayons **sit**" instead of "sits"); two subjects joined by *and* take a singular verb ("Quentin and Rosalind **plays**"); *there* is the subject in "There is" and "There are," so the verb never changes; an -s on the verb means plural, the way it does on a noun (so "The bird **sing**" looks right). Reteach: "Cross out the prepositional phrase, find the real subject, and say the two together: 'The box sits.'"
- Also fair to test: *or* and *nor* ("Neither the coach nor the players **are** late"; the verb agrees with the closer subject); indefinite pronouns whose number is settled (*everyone*, *somebody*, and *nobody* are singular, and so is *each* when it is the subject, as in "Each of the twins **has** a bike"; never test *each* after a plural subject, as in "The twins each **have** a bike," where the verb is plural; *both*, *few*, *many*, and *several* are plural).
- Hazards: **Never build an item on** a collective noun or a "group of" phrase where writers go both ways ("A group of kids **is**" or "**are**," "The team **is**" or "**are**" in a sentence about separate members); *none* with a plural noun ("None of the cookies **is**" or "**are**"), since both are standard; *all*, *some*, *most*, and *half*, which take their number from the noun after *of*, unless that noun settles it; a compound that names one thing ("Macaroni and cheese **is**"); *data* or *media*; *there's* before a plural, which is common in speech; and *there is* or *there are* before a compound ("There ___ a pencil and two erasers"), where *is* is standard. **Only in this pinned form:** a *there is* or *there are* item has a single noun after the verb, singular or plural ("There ___ three erasers on the desk"). **Only in this pinned form:** the stem must pin the tense, or every option must be in the same tense. "My sister ___ to school every day" with options *walk*, *walks*, and *walked* has two right answers, because *walked* agrees with any subject; offer *walk* and *walks* only. At grades 1-2 an agreement fill-in may have just those two options; never pad it with a filler third option like *walking*. If the options must differ in tense, the stem needs a time word that rules out every tense but one (*tomorrow* for *will walk*).

**Complete sentences, fragments, and run-ons**
- Misconceptions: long means complete and short means fragment ("Stop." is complete); a dependent clause is a sentence ("When the bus pulled away."); a run-on is a long sentence (a short one can be a run-on: "I ran I won."); a comma fixes a run-on. Reteach: "A sentence needs a subject, a verb, and a complete thought; length has nothing to do with it."
- Hazards: a run-on can be fixed several ways (a period; a semicolon; a semicolon with a conjunctive adverb and a comma, as in "; however,"; a comma plus a FANBOYS word; a subordinating conjunction; or, when the second clause explains or illustrates the first, a colon), so the key accepts all of them unless the item names the method; commands are complete.

**Sentence types**
- Misconceptions: the end mark decides the type (a command with an exclamation point is still a command); any sentence with feeling is exclamatory. Reteach: "The type is the sentence's job: telling, asking, commanding, or exclaiming."
- Hazards: "Watch out!" is a command that ends with an exclamation point, and curricula classify it differently; avoid sentences where job and end mark disagree at grades 1-2, or state the rule the class uses.

**Sentence structures**
- Misconceptions: any *and* makes a sentence compound (misses compound subjects and predicates in a simple sentence); complex means long or hard; a compound sentence can be joined with a comma alone. Reteach: "Count the independent clauses and the dependent ones: one independent clause is simple; two or more is compound; one independent clause plus at least one dependent clause is complex; two or more independent clauses plus at least one dependent clause is compound-complex."
- Hazards: relative clauses (*who*, *which*, *that*) make a sentence complex; imperatives with two clauses are easy to misread; make each option differ in exactly one structural way.

**Sentence combining**
- Misconceptions: combining means joining with *and*; combining sentences can drop information. Reteach: "Combine to show how ideas relate, and keep every fact."
- Hazards: many combinations are correct, so the key states criteria (one sentence, every fact kept, correct punctuation, a conjunction that fits the meaning) and lists two or three acceptable versions.

**Objects and complements**
- Misconceptions: the noun after any verb is a direct object (misses predicate nominatives after linking verbs); the object of a preposition is an indirect object ("gave the book **to Sam**"). Reteach: "An indirect object sits between the verb and the direct object with no preposition: 'gave **Sam** the book.'"
- Hazards: verbs that can be linking or action; keep to clear transitive verbs (*gave*, *handed*, *sent*, *built*).

**Phrases and verbals**
- Misconceptions: every *-ing* word is a verb; gerunds and participles are the same thing (a gerund works as a noun, a participle as an adjective); *to* plus a verb is a prepositional phrase; a modifier can go anywhere in a sentence (dangling and misplaced modifiers). Reteach: "Ask what job the verb form is doing: naming something (gerund), describing something (participle), or, for an infinitive, working as a noun, an adjective, or an adverb."
- Hazards: "She is **walking** the puppy" is a verb phrase, not a verbal, but "Her favorite chore is **walking**" is a gerund, so the stem must pin the role (see the *-ing* hazard under action verbs); some *-ing* words have become ordinary nouns or adjectives (*building*, *interesting*), so choose clearer examples.

**Clauses**
- Misconceptions: any group of words is a clause (a clause needs its own subject and verb); every clause starting with *that* is dependent; a dependent clause can stand alone if it's long. Reteach: "A clause has its own subject and verb; it's independent only if it can stand alone as a complete thought."
- Hazards: *that* can be a pronoun, a demonstrative adjective, or the word that introduces a clause; noun clauses as subjects ("What she said surprised me") are hard below grade 7.

## The Writing Company

- Misconceptions: a fact is an opinion if the writer agrees with it; a topic sentence is any first sentence; transitions are interchangeable (*however* used for addition, *for example* used for contrast); a conclusion repeats the first sentence word for word; a detail supports a reason if it's about the same topic. Reteach: "Each transition has a job: adding, contrasting, giving an example, showing a result, or showing time."
- Hazards: "best" questions (best hook, best ending, best word choice) have more than one defensible answer; opinion versus fact needs clear-cut sentences ("Dogs are the best pets." versus "Dogs have four legs."); sequence items need events with one logical order.

## Wonderful Words

- Misconceptions: synonyms mean exactly the same thing (misses shades of meaning: *glance* versus *stare*); an antonym is any different word; any word that starts with the same letters as a prefix has that prefix (reading "un" in *under* or "re" in *ready* as a prefix, when those letters are part of the root); a word has only one meaning; context clues are just the words next to the blank. Reteach for prefixes: "Cover the letters. If what's left isn't a word or a root with a meaning, those letters weren't a prefix." Reteach for shades of meaning: "Words in the same family are related but not twins; line them up from weakest to strongest."
- Hazards: key "closest in meaning," never "means the same"; avoid words with regional meanings; homographs need a sentence that settles which meaning; don't test words a grade is unlikely to know unless context makes them solvable; a teacher's unit words come from the teacher.
