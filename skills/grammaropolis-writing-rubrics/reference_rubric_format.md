# Reference: Rubric Format (composition rules)

*The composition rules every Grammaropolis rubric follows. These rules follow the Grammaropolis Writing Company rubric format, which grew out of real middle-school classroom rubrics.*

---

## The tier structure

Four tiers, always, at every grade and in every mode:

**Gold / Silver / Bronze / Not Yet**

1. **Same tier names at every grade.** A student moving from Grade 3 to Grade 4 knows what Silver feels like; the bar moves, the language does not. Never substitute numeric scores, letter grades, or star ratings.
2. **"Not Yet" is an invitation, not a failure.** Every Not Yet descriptor and every Mayor Not Yet reaction frames revision as the next step. The register: "Not yet. Let me show you what is missing, and then you write it again." Never punitive, never final.
3. **Tier logic runs strong-to-developing, left to right.** Gold is the fully realized move; Silver is the move present but improvable; Bronze is the move attempted; Not Yet is the move absent or off-mode.
4. **Descriptors are specific, not evaluative.** "Title takes a clear position; the reader knows your stance from the headline alone" beats "Excellent title." A descriptor tells the writer what the work DOES at that tier, so the writer can see which tier their own work matches. Banned as bare evaluations in descriptors: excellent, good, great, poor, bad, weak.
5. **Descriptors are complete sentences.** Subject, verb, and complement. No fragments ("Some errors" is not a descriptor; "A few errors appear, but they do not interfere with meaning" is).
6. **Binary categories may skip tiers.** A Power Move category at grades where it is optional gets Gold and Silver descriptors only (earned or attempted). The Format category may carry only Gold and Not Yet descriptors. When a tier cell is deliberately empty, leave it empty.

## The category convention

1. **At most 8 rows by default, counting the closing rows.** A teacher grading a paragraph needs a rubric that fits on a page. Keep the mode's most important categories for the grade (the grade's required ones always stay: Counterclaim at Grades 6-8 persuasive, the sourcing convention at Grades 7-8), fold related categories into one row where they belong together (Title and Opening Hook; Reasons and Facts and Details), and let Mechanics and Format share one Conventions row when space is short. Grades 1-2 need only about 4 mode-specific rows. Offer the **full version**, every category the kit lists for the grade (up to about 12-13 mode-specific rows at Grades 6-8), on request. Full-version counts per grade are in `reference_grade_calibration.md`.
2. **Categories scale by addition, not replacement.** Earlier-grade categories never disappear; later grades add categories on top, and some names mature (Opinion Statement becomes Claim Statement at Grade 6). A teacher who learns the rubric at one grade can apply it to neighboring grades with predictable adjustments.
3. **Mode-specific categories first, the standard closing rows last.** Every rubric closes with, in order (Mechanics and Format may share a Conventions row in the default version): **Sentences** (variety, structure, active voice), **Mechanics** (capitalization, punctuation, spelling), **Format** (assignment formatting, grade-appropriate: typed conventions at upper grades, handwriting accommodation at Grade 4 and below).
4. **Each category carries its CCSS citation** in parentheses and italics next to the category name: `**Opinion Statement** *(W.5.1.A)*`. Mechanics and Format may go uncited. Every citation must appear verbatim in `reference_ccss_writing_standards.md`.
5. **Power Move categories** are labeled `Power Move: [Name]` with their status inline: `*(optional at Grade 5; required Grade 6 and up; W.7.1.A)*`. The Power Move is the stretch category that previews upper-grade craft without penalizing at-grade work.
6. **Category names calibrate to grade.** "What I Think" at Grades 1-2, "Opinion Statement" at Grades 3-5, "Claim Statement" at Grades 6-8.

## The table layout

One markdown table: category column plus four tier columns.

```
| Category | Gold | Silver | Bronze | Not Yet |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **[Category Name]** *(CCSS code)* | [descriptor] | [descriptor] | [descriptor] | [descriptor] |
```

Categories run vertically; tiers run horizontally. One row per category. No merged cells.

## The framing quote

Every rubric opens with a Mayor framing quote, italicized, directly under the title: 2-4 sentences that (a) frame the mode as a purposeful act, (b) name the mode's core moves in two or three beats, and (c) close on the Mayor's productive formality. The standard example:

> *"A good persuasive paragraph is a small act of leadership. You take a position, you give your reasons, and you stand by them, with civility. The Mayor will accept nothing less."*

The framing quote and the tier-reactions section are the ONLY places Mayor voice flourishes appear. Tier descriptors stay in plain, encouraging, specific teacher language.

## "How the Mayor reads the four tiers"

After the table: four short paragraphs, one per tier. Each has (1) a plain description of what a whole piece at this tier reads like, and (2) the Mayor's reaction line, italicized, introduced by "The Mayor reads it and thinks:". Open the section with: "The Mayor evaluates the whole paragraph, not just individual categories." Reaction reference lines and construction rules are in `reference_mayor_voice_card.md`.

## CCSS standards alignment summary

A closing section mapping each category to its primary standard, plus a secondary-alignment note covering W.X.4 (clear and coherent writing), L.X.3 (knowledge of language), and L.X.6 (vocabulary). If a Power Move previews an upper-grade standard, say so and name the grade where it becomes required.

## The signature line

Every teacher rubric closes with:

```
The Writing Company certifies this work as:    ___ Gold    ___ Silver    ___ Bronze    ___ Not Yet
```

The certifying voice is always the Mayor.

## Output variants

The full tiered rubric is a **teacher artifact**. The student-facing companion is a positive checklist with no tiers, so students check their own work against moves they can see rather than against grades.

| Variant | Includes | Excludes |
|---|---|---|
| Teacher rubric (default) | Title, Mayor framing quote, category × tier table, How the Mayor reads the four tiers, CCSS alignment summary, signature line | Comments box (offer on request) |
| Student self-check ("Before You Turn It In") | One warm Mayor opening line, 6-8 yes/no self-check items derived from the most actionable categories, one page | Tier names, tier descriptors, CCSS codes, any grading criteria |
| Reference card | Mayor framing quote, category × tier table, one teacher use-note, the main standard cited once in the header | Alignment summary detail, signature line, comments |

Checklist derivation rules: pick the 6-8 categories a student can self-verify (presence checks, not quality judgments: "Did I say what I think?" yes; "Is my hook vivid?" no); phrase each as a first-person yes/no question; never leak tier language (the student checklist has no Gold, Silver, Bronze, or Not Yet).
