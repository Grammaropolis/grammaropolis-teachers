# The Grammaropolis teaching cycle

Every Grammaropolis lesson, print or digital, runs the same five-step cycle. This file explains each step for a teacher audience: what it's for, how long it runs, and what it looks like in a real classroom. Use it to fill in the cycle structure in `reference_plan_template.md`.

The cycle is not five equally-sized chunks of time. Learn and Practice usually take the most minutes; Assess and the Certified step are quick by design, so a teacher never loses the period to testing.

## Before the cycle: the Hook

Not one of the five steps, but every plan opens with one. A character walks in and makes the day's concept impossible to ignore before any instruction starts. **1-3 minutes.** In the classroom: read the character's line aloud, project the character's image if you have it, or just tell the class in your own words what the character would say. The hook's only job is to make a student curious enough to want the answer that Learn is about to give them.

## 1. Learn

**What it's for:** direct instruction in the concept, delivered in the voice of the character who owns it. **10-15 minutes**, more for a concept with several sub-parts (Nelson's six kinds of nouns takes longer than a single-idea concept). In the classroom: this is the part of the period that looks like teaching. Model the concept with 2-3 clear examples, in the character's voice if you're comfortable doing the character (optional; the concept teaches fine straight too), then check for understanding with a quick show of hands or a turn-and-talk before moving on. A Grammaropolis Learn step never front-loads terminology the students don't need yet; it teaches through examples first, names the rule second.

## 2. Assess

**What it's for:** a quick, low-stakes check, not a test. In a lesson plan it's 3-5 questions, optionally reported in four tiers: Gold (every item right), Silver (at least two-thirds right), Bronze (at least half right), and Not Yet (less than half right, so the class needs another pass at Learn before Practice). **3-5 minutes.** In the classroom: this can be an exit-ticket-style set of questions shown on the board and read aloud, answered with hand signals, a quick written check, or a verbal cold-call round. If an item depends on something students must see (a capital letter, a comma, a spelling), write it where they can see it; never ask them to judge a mark they've only heard. The tiers are a report to the teacher, not a grade for the student; frame it as "let's see where we are," never as a quiz worth points. A lesson plan drafted by this skill compresses this into ONE exit-ticket-style question set, since a full classroom period doesn't have room for the multi-question digital version.

## 3. Practice

**What it's for:** low-stakes repetition once the concept has landed, in a form built to feel like play rather than drill: sorting words into buckets, matching pairs, filling in a blank, or (for the youngest grades) an interactive story where the right answer moves the plot forward. **10-15 minutes.** In the classroom: this is the worksheet-replacement step, best run as partner work, a physical sort (word cards on desks), or a whole-class call-and-response. No new content here, only reps on what Learn already taught. If a class is short on time, Practice is the step to shorten, not Learn or Create.

## 4. Create

**What it's for:** the student uses the concept in their own original writing or speech, not just recognizing it in someone else's sentence. Every Create step ends with a short student-authored piece: a sentence, a few sentences, or (for Writing Company lessons) a short paragraph. **10-20 minutes** depending on grade and the length of the ask. In the classroom: give the prompt, let students write independently, then invite 2-3 volunteers to share aloud. This is the step where a teacher hears whether the concept actually transferred.

## 5. Certified

**What it's for:** naming what the student can now do that they couldn't do at the start of the period. **1-2 minutes.** In the classroom: say it out loud, plainly ("You can now tell a common noun from a proper one, every time.") A print classroom can hand out a small certificate or sticker if one exists for the concept; a spoken closing works just as well. This is also the one place a plan may point to grammaropolis.com (the free lesson for a free topic, the department's page otherwise; see the closing-pointer rule): never more than once, and never as a sales pitch.

## Timing a full period around the cycle

Use the split for the period length; the minutes add up exactly.

| Step | 30 minutes (the default) | 45 minutes |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | 2 | 2 |
| Learn | 9 | 12 |
| Assess | 3 | 5 |
| Practice | 7 | 12 |
| Create | 7 | 12 |
| Certified | 2 | 2 |
| **Total** | **30** | **45** |

For any other length, start from the nearer split and move minutes in or out of Practice first, then Create; the Hook and Certified stay at 2. If time is short, protect Learn and Create, since those are where the teaching and the evidence of learning actually happen.

## Grade-band notes

- **Grades 1-2:** shorten Assess to 2-3 questions and keep Practice highly concrete (physical sorting, drawing, pointing) rather than abstract classification. Create is often one sentence, sometimes with a sentence starter.
- **Grades 3-5:** the full five-step shape runs comfortably in one period. Practice can introduce light abstraction (matching, cloze) alongside concrete sorting.
- **Grades 6-8:** Learn can move faster (older students need fewer worked examples), which buys extra time for Practice or Create. Assess can include a "why" justification, not just a right answer, since older students can articulate reasoning.
