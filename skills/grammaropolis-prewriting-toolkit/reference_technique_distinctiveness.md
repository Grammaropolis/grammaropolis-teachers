# Reference: Technique Distinctiveness Checklist

*Check 1 of the four quality checks. Each of the 8 prewriting techniques must produce a structurally distinctive output. This file specifies the structural signature each output must hit and the failure mode to catch.*

*The technique definitions and structural signatures come from the Grammaropolis Writing Framework, whose worked examples establish what each technique's output must look like.*

*The key insight: a freewrite that reads like a brainstorm in paragraph form is a failed worked example, even if every individual sentence is fine. The pedagogical point is that DIFFERENT techniques produce DIFFERENT shapes. If two outputs look the same shape, the skill has failed Check 1.*

---

## The 8 distinctive signatures

### 1. Freewrite

**Standard definition**: Stream of consciousness. Put pen to paper and see where your brain takes you. Useful when you don't know what to talk about and need to generate potential ideas.

**Structural signature**:
- Continuous prose, not list form
- Meandering: at least one tangential drift away from the central topic
- Conversational tone with first-person interiority
- May end mid-thought or with an associative leap
- Sentence-by-sentence chain of association, not logical argument

**The Garvin reference freewrite drifts** (from the framework's worked example):
- Starts with the prompt ("There have only been a couple of times when I have been so proud...")
- Drifts into philosophical territory ("Some people think that being proud of yourself is bragging...")
- Returns to the source incident ("I was at my friend Garvin's house...")
- Drifts into adjacent territory ("We did lots of other stuff together though, like riding bikes...")
- Ends with the dog Stella, who is not in the source scene at all

**Failure modes to catch**:
- The output reads like an organized argument (FAIL: that is closer to a Cluster's output structure)
- The output reads like a list of discrete moments (FAIL: that is the Brainstorm's structure)
- The output stays rigidly on the source story without any drift (FAIL: missing the meander signature)
- The output uses bullet points, numbered lists, or labeled sections (FAIL: those are list-form structures, not stream-of-consciousness)
- The meander is carried by chaining clauses onto one long sentence with "and," "but," "so," or "because" (FAIL: this is the single most common way a Freewrite blows its grade's sentence-length ceiling in `reference_grade_calibration.md`; meander across MORE sentences, not longer ones. Check every sentence in the draft individually against the grade's maximum word count before delivering, not just the total word count or sentence count)

**Length conventions per grade**:
- 1: 2-4 short sentences with one drift
- 2: 3-5 short sentences with one drift
- 3: 4-6 sentences with one clear drift
- 4: 5-8 sentences with one or two drifts
- 5: 7-12 sentences with multiple drifts (Garvin reference level)
- 6: 8-13 sentences with multiple drifts and tighter associative logic
- 7: 9-14 sentences with sophisticated associative leaps
- 8: 10-15 sentences with elevated abstraction

These are sentence COUNTS, not a license on sentence LENGTH: every sentence in a Freewrite still has to individually clear the grade's average and maximum words-per-sentence targets in `reference_grade_calibration.md`. A Freewrite that hits its sentence count by chaining two or three thoughts into one long compound sentence has not actually hit the grade's calibration, even though the count looks right.

**A worked contrast, the fix in practice (Grade 4, maximum 20 words per sentence).** This is the most common failure, so do it as an explicit written step:

- **WRONG** (one chained sentence, 34 words, blows the Grade 4 maximum even though it reads as "one Freewrite sentence"): "I was so nervous when we started cooking and my hands were shaking because I had never used the stove by myself before and Garvin kept telling me it would be fine."
- **RIGHT** (same content, same meandering drift, same conversational voice, split into four short sentences, each individually under 20 words): "I was so nervous when we started cooking. My hands were shaking. I had never used the stove by myself before. Garvin kept telling me it would be fine."

The fix is never to trim words out of the long sentence (that flattens the conversational voice); it is to split it at each "and"/"but"/"so"/"because" into its own sentence. The meander survives the split; only the run-on doesn't.

### 2. Brainstorm

**Standard definition**: If it pops into your head, it goes on the page. You may not actually use most of what you generate. The storm is what's important.

**Structural signature**:
- Discrete list items, not full sentences
- Multiple parallel central topics acceptable (the Garvin reference brainstorm includes "patted myself on the back," "taught myself a handstand," and "gave my sister my coat" as parallel candidate stories)
- Variety beats depth: better to have 8 different ideas than 4 deep ones
- No structural ordering implied (the list is unordered)
- Items can be fragments, phrases, or short sentences

**The Garvin reference brainstorm output** has three parallel central topics (the anniversary dinner, the handstand, the coat gift) with 3-5 items per topic. This shows the student that brainstorm can be wider than they expect.

**Failure modes to catch**:
- The output is full sentences in a flowing structure (FAIL: that is the Freewrite's structure)
- All items relate only to the chosen source story (FAIL: missing the variety signature; the student should see that brainstorm explores)
- Items are organized into categories or labeled (FAIL: that is the Cluster's structure)
- Items are arranged chronologically (FAIL: that is the Series of Events' structure)

**Length conventions per grade**:
- 1: 5-7 short items, single central topic
- 2: 6-9 items, possibly 2 central topics
- 3: 8-11 items across 1-2 central topics
- 4: 10-14 items across 1-2 central topics
- 5: 12-18 items across 2-3 central topics (Garvin reference level)
- 6: 13-20 items across 2-3 central topics
- 7: 15-22 items across 2-4 central topics
- 8: 17-25 items across 2-4 central topics

### 3. Cluster

**Standard definition**: A type of organized brainstorm. A cluster will help show the relationships between your ideas and what might need to be developed more.

**Structural signature**:
- Visual or text representation of a central node
- Radiating connected ideas surround the central idea
- Spatial relationships matter: items near each other in the cluster are conceptually related
- Lines or proximity connect related items
- The reader should be able to see grouping at a glance

**The Garvin reference cluster** uses a central node ("Cooked dinner for Garvin's parents") with about 10 radiating items including "Garvin's parents great cooks," "I was nervous," "Eggs for dinner," "We spilled egg yolk," "smoky in the kitchen," etc. The items are arranged spatially around the center.

**Failure modes to catch**:
- The output is a flat list (FAIL: that is the Brainstorm's structure)
- The output has no central node (FAIL: missing the structural skeleton)
- Items have no spatial or grouping relationship (FAIL: it is just a bulleted brainstorm in disguise)
- Items are in 6 labeled categories (FAIL: that is closer to the 5W & H structure)

**Output format**:
- Worked-example set: text representation using indentation and headers to suggest the radiating structure, or use code-block ASCII layout
- Demonstration script: narrator describes the cluster step by step ("I started with the center: 'Cooking dinner for Garvin's parents.' Then I drew lines out to the things that mattered most...")

**Length conventions per grade**:
- 1: Central node plus 4-5 items
- 2: Central node plus 5-7 items
- 3: Central node plus 6-8 items
- 4: Central node plus 7-10 items
- 5: Central node plus 10-15 items (Garvin reference level)
- 6: Central node plus 11-16 items, possibly one sub-cluster
- 7: Central node plus 12-18 items, possibly with sub-clusters
- 8: Central node plus 14-20 items with multi-level sub-clusters

### 4. 5W & H

**Standard definition**: The Journalist's Questions. A way to generate information you already know and identify what you need to find out. Ideal for informative or explanatory writing.

**Structural signature**:
- Six fixed labeled slots: Who, What, Where, When, Why, How
- Each slot has factual content (not narrative, not opinion)
- Slots may be 1 sentence each or multi-sentence with multiple sub-items
- No prose connecting the slots; each is independent

**The Garvin reference 5W & H output** includes specific facts: "Who: my friend Garvin, his parents Mr. & Mrs. James, me." "What: fried eggs, mashed potatoes, green beans, fresh carrots, snap peas, hummus." Each slot is factual and self-contained.

**Failure modes to catch**:
- The output is a flowing narrative (FAIL: that is the Freewrite's structure)
- The slots include opinion or emotional content (FAIL: 5W & H is fact-only)
- One or more slots is missing (FAIL: the 6-slot structure is required)
- Slots are not labeled (FAIL: the labels are part of the technique's signature)

**Length conventions per grade**:
- 1: 3W (Who, What, Where) with one-sentence answers per slot
- 2: Full 5W & H with one-sentence answers per slot
- 3: Full 5W & H with one-to-two-sentence answers per slot
- 4: Full 5W & H with one-or-two-item answers per slot
- 5: Full 5W & H with multi-item answers per slot (Garvin reference level)
- 6: Full 5W & H with multi-item answers per slot and analytical H
- 7: Full 5W & H with detailed multi-item answers and analytical H questions
- 8: Full 5W & H with detailed multi-item answers, analytical H, and possible follow-up sub-questions

### 5. Venn Diagram

**Standard definition**: A type of structured brainstorm that shows what the similarities and differences are between two subjects. Particularly useful for compare-and-contrast purposes.

**Structural signature**:
- Two distinct subjects from within the source story being compared
- Three regions: A-only (left circle), B-only (right circle), overlap (both)
- Each region has at least 3-5 items
- Items are factual or observational, not narrative

**The Garvin reference Venn diagram** compares "Cooking Fried Eggs" vs. "Cooking Mashed Potatoes" (two sub-topics from within the source story, not the source story vs. something external). A-only includes "easy to mess up, sizzling sound, takes a minute." B-only includes "fun to mash, gurgling sound, takes an hour." Overlap includes "cooked on stove, Mr. James loves, salt."

**Failure modes to catch**:
- The comparison is between the source story and something external (FAIL: the standard pattern is internal comparison)
- The output is just two parallel lists (FAIL: missing the overlap region)
- One region dominates the others (FAIL: rough balance expected)
- The output is a flowing comparison paragraph (FAIL: that is the Compare/Contrast writing mode, not the prewriting technique)

**Output format**:
- Worked-example set: three labeled blocks (A-only, Overlap, B-only) using headers or bullet sections, or use code-block ASCII Venn representation
- Demonstration script: narrator describes filling in each region

**Length conventions per grade**:
- 1: same/different two-column layout; 2-3 items per column (4-6 total)
- 2: introduce overlapping-circles Venn; 2-3 items per region (6-9 total)
- 3: 3 items per region (9 total)
- 4: 3-4 items per region (9-12 total)
- 5: 4-6 items per region (12-18 total)
- 6: 4-6 items per region (12-18 total), with nuanced overlap criteria
- 7: 5-7 items per region (15-21 total)
- 8: 5-7 items per region (15-21 total), with sophisticated overlap criteria

### 6. Five Senses

**Standard definition**: Information developed with your five senses is wonderful for generating material for descriptive writing.

**Structural signature**:
- Five fixed labeled slots: See, Hear, Taste, Smell, Touch
- Each slot has 2-4 sensory details
- Each detail is specific and concrete (not "good" or "nice")
- No narrative connecting slots; each is independent
- All details point back to the source scene

**The Garvin reference Five Senses output**: "See: Bright kitchen, white countertop, 4-burner stove. Total mess on the counter, egg whites, broken yolk." "Hear: sizzling of the egg in the frying pan, clinking of potato masher against the pot, bubbling sound of boiling water." Each sense slot is specific.

**Failure modes to catch**:
- The output is a flowing descriptive paragraph (FAIL: that is the Descriptive writing mode, not the prewriting technique)
- Details are non-sensory (e.g., "we were nervous" in the See slot) (FAIL: that is interior, not sensory)
- One or more slots is missing (FAIL: 5-slot structure required)
- Details are too generic ("looks good, sounds nice") (FAIL: specificity is the point)

**Length conventions per grade**:
- 1: 1-2 details per sense (5-10 total)
- 2: 2 details per sense (10 total)
- 3: 2-3 details per sense (10-15 total)
- 4: 2-3 details per sense (10-15 total)
- 5: 3-4 details per sense (15-20 total)
- 6: 3-4 details per sense (15-20 total)
- 7: 3-5 details per sense (15-25 total)
- 8: 4-5 details per sense (20-25 total)

### 7. Series of Events

**Standard definition**: Before you start to tell a story, list the things that happened so that you don't forget any important parts and that your events are in the right order.

**Structural signature**:
- Chronological list of discrete events
- No sensory details (that's the Five Senses' job)
- No interior opinion or stake (that's the Freewrite's job)
- No characterization (that's the Character Sketch's job)
- Each event is observable, external, and time-positioned

**The Garvin reference Series of Events**: "I went to Garvin's house after school. We decided to do something special for Garvin's parents. We rode our bikes to the store. Garvin squeezed the egg too hard when he was trying to crack it, and the egg white went everywhere. Garvin admitted that he had never fried an egg." Each item is a discrete event in time order.

**Failure modes to catch**:
- Events include sensory details (FAIL: cross-pollination with Five Senses)
- Events include narrator's feelings (FAIL: cross-pollination with Freewrite)
- Events include character traits (FAIL: cross-pollination with Character Sketch)
- Order is not chronological (FAIL: the time-ordering is structural)
- The output is a flowing narrative (FAIL: that is the Narrative writing mode)

**Length conventions per grade**:
- 1: 3-4 events
- 2: 4-5 events
- 3: 5-7 events
- 4: 6-9 events
- 5: 8-13 events (Garvin reference level; the Garvin source story lists 14 events, so choose the most important 8-13)
- 6: 9-13 events
- 7: 10-14 events, possibly with sub-events
- 8: 11-15 events, with sub-events

### 8. Character Sketch

**Standard definition**: Spending time thinking about your characters is a great way to develop your story and make it more interesting.

**Structural signature**:
- Bulleted inventory of one character's traits
- Mix of physical traits, personality traits, history, contradictions, and quirks
- Not the narrator's traits (those are the narrator's stake, not a Character Sketch)
- Specific details, not generic ("loves Pokemon cards he never looks at" beats "likes games")
- May include traits that won't appear in the final draft (the point is to develop the character internally)

**The Garvin reference Character Sketch** of Garvin: "Has been my best friend since we were three years old. Loves chocolate and cheeseburgers, in that order. Short, curly hair and brown eyes. Three inches taller than me. Wants to be a professional basketball player but is a much better singer than a basketball player. Once tripped on the curb and knocked his front teeth. Has a huge collection of Pokemon cards he never actually looks at. Goofy. Gets super angry when you eat his dessert when he isn't looking. Absolutely loves to play practical jokes on people."

Note the variety: physical (curly hair, brown eyes, three inches taller), aspirational (basketball player), historical (front teeth knock), quirky (Pokemon cards never used), social (gets angry about dessert), behavioral (loves pranks). The mix of dimensions is the signature.

**Failure modes to catch**:
- All traits are physical (FAIL: missing the personality dimension)
- All traits are personality (FAIL: missing the physical and historical dimensions)
- The character has no contradictions (FAIL: "wants to be X but is actually Y better" is the standard move)
- The traits are generic ("nice, smart, funny") (FAIL: specificity is the point)
- The output is about the narrator (FAIL: Character Sketch is about another character, not self)

**Length conventions per grade**:
- 1: 4-5 traits across 2-3 dimensions
- 2: 5-6 traits across 3 dimensions
- 3: 6-7 traits across 3-4 dimensions
- 4: 7-8 traits across 4 dimensions
- 5: 8-10 traits across 4-5 dimensions (Garvin reference level: 10 traits)
- 6: 9-11 traits with at least one contradiction
- 7: 10-12 traits with explicit contradiction
- 8: 11-13 traits with multiple contradictions and internal complexity

## Cross-technique distinctiveness summary

A clean worked-example set produces 8 outputs that look like 8 different things. The reader, looking at all 8 side by side, should see:

| Technique | Visual signature |
|---|---|
| Freewrite | Flowing prose paragraph |
| Brainstorm | Flat list of fragments |
| Cluster | Radial diagram with center + spokes |
| 5W & H | Six labeled Q&A slots |
| Venn Diagram | Two overlapping circles with three regions |
| Five Senses | Five labeled sense slots with details |
| Series of Events | Chronological numbered list |
| Character Sketch | One-character trait inventory |

If two of the visual signatures look the same in the output set, the skill has failed Check 1.

## The skill's self-check question

After generating all 8 outputs, the skill asks: "If a student in the target grade saw these 8 outputs side by side, could they identify which technique produced which output without reading the labels?"

If yes for all 8, Check 1 passes. If no for any, that output is rewritten.
