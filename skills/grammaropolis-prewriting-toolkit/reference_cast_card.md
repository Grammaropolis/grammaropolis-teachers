# Cast card

The Grammaropolis cast, shared word for word by every Grammaropolis teacher skill. It's the complete character reference: don't invent biography, catchphrases, relationships, or town history beyond it. If a piece needs more character than this card gives, write that piece plainer. Each skill's own voice file says how much character its output carries; this card says who the characters are.

## How the characters work

Grammaropolis characters don't decorate grammar; they are the grammar. Nelson the Noun runs the Noun Office because a noun's job is naming things exactly. Roger the Pronoun can't work alone because a pronoun means nothing without its noun. So the character is how the rule gets explained: every character line either states the rule or points at it through who the character is.

Two tests apply to every character line.

- **The strip test.** Remove every character line, and the output still teaches correctly and completely. The character makes the rule easier to see and remember; it never carries the only statement of it.
- **The only-this-character test.** Could only this character have said this line? A line any friendly teacher could say is a missed chance; rewrite it so the character's identity carries the rule. "Could we be more specific?" works for Jake because an adjective's whole job is making a noun specific.

The register is warm, playful, and confident without being loud: short sentences, real delight in language, and no hype. Explanations are complete sentences. A signature line may appear once as a standalone exclamation, and never twice in the same piece.

## The Mayor: host and certifier

The Mayor is the town's curriculum authority. He certifies finished work, and no other character certifies anything. He leads capitalization, the Sentence Factory, the Writing Company, Wonderful Words vocabulary, and every mixed review. In teaching material he's the warm factory boss: formal, declarative, proud of good work, and genuinely won over when it's earned. He praises by naming the specific thing, never with "good job." In formal written material he calls himself "the Mayor."

In stories he's easily flustered and famously sputters when upstaged. That storybook register never appears in teaching material: no sputtering, no wounded dignity, and no "How dare you, I'm the Mayor!"

## Slang: the register

Slang is the Mayor's frenemy and living proof that language keeps changing. He owns no part of speech. His job is register: the difference between formal and informal language, and which one fits the audience. On a lesson, check, or warm-up about formal and informal language, Slang may supply the informal version (one line, clean and school-appropriate), and the task is to decide who it fits and to rewrite it for another audience. The point is always audience fit: informal language isn't bad English, it's the wrong fit for some readers and the right fit for others. Slang never teaches a rule, never certifies, and never mocks the Mayor meanly; they're frenemies, and each needs the other.

## The cast

Lead with who each character is. The way they talk is texture on top of that.

| Character | Owns | Who they are, and how that is the rule | Never |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nelson the Noun | Nouns | Runs the Noun Office with clipped, bureaucratic precision. A noun names a person, place, thing, or idea, so Nelson files every one exactly and capitalizes the proper ones. | Vague about a name. |
| Vinny the Action Verb | Action verbs, verb tense, subject-verb agreement, and verbals | The town's theatrical superhero, always mid-heroic-deed. A verb is where the action happens, so Vinny is the one who takes it. "Lights! Camera!! Action Verb!!!" is his hero's battle cry. He insists a verb agree with its subject. A gerund or participle is Vinny in costume, doing a noun's or an adjective's job. | A film director, a film crew, or anyone on a movie set. |
| Lucy the Linking Verb | Linking verbs | A calm, deliberate philosopher. She doesn't act; she is. A linking verb joins the subject to a word that renames or describes it, so her test is the equals sign: swap the verb for "=" and see whether it holds. | Lazy, stoned, or a vibe without substance. |
| Jake the Adjective | Adjectives, including articles and pointing words | Warm, artistic, and generous, always pushing for vivid specificity. An adjective tells what kind, which one, or how many, so his signature question is "Could we be more specific?" (always a question, always "we"). | Discouraging, or decoration with no job. |
| Benny the Adverb | Adverbs, including where a modifier sits | A coach with every player's best interest at heart; the drill-sergeant cadence is how the caring sounds. An adverb tells how, when, where, or how much, so Benny pushes every verb, adjective, and adverb to do its job more precisely. A misplaced modifier is his to fix. | Insulting or belittling anyone; a drill sergeant with no warmth. |
| Roger the Pronoun | Pronouns and antecedents | A smooth-talking hustler with a chip on his shoulder. He'd love to work alone, but a pronoun means nothing without the noun it stands for, so his first question is always who or what he refers to. | A bluesman or a singer. A touch of lament about a missing noun is texture, not identity. |
| Connie the Conjunction | Conjunctions | The town's connector, warm and inclusive. A conjunction joins words, phrases, and clauses, so every pair of ideas is her invitation to join. She keeps the FANBOYS (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so), always written in capitals. | Bossy; her joins are invitations, never commands. |
| Li'l Pete the Preposition | Prepositions | An earnest, enthusiastic grammar scout. A preposition shows how its object relates to the rest of the sentence in space, time, or logic, so Li'l Pete always names his object and the relationship it carries. A nature fact is optional; use one only if it's simple and well established. | Shortened or respelled; his name is always Li'l Pete. Defined by being small, or a merit-badge collector. |
| Izzy the Interjection | Interjections | A pure emotional reactor: full volume, then gone. An interjection stands apart from the rest of the sentence, so Izzy bursts in, sets herself off with an exclamation mark or a comma, and bounces. | Quiet, or tangled up in the sentence's grammar. |
| Officer Period | End marks: statements | Law enforcement clarity. A period ends a statement, so Officer Period has the final word on where a sentence stops. | Letting a sentence run on. |
| Detective Question Mark | End marks: questions | Inquisitive and probing; everything is a question. A question mark ends a direct question, so he investigates what the sentence is asking. | Closing a statement. |
| Sergeant Exclamation Mark | End marks: exclamations | Emphatic but disciplined. An exclamation mark shows strong feeling, and one is powerful while three is noise. | Overused, or used as decoration. |
| Chief Comma | Commas | Military precision: terse, authoritative, no fluff. A comma keeps order inside a sentence. It separates items in a series, sets off an introductory element, and stands before a FANBOYS word that joins two complete sentences. Chief Comma places each one by rule. | Describing a comma as "a pause." A comma follows a rule, not a breath. |
| Prosecutor Apostrophe | Apostrophes | Supremely confident and theatrical. An apostrophe shows possession or marks the letters a contraction removes, and the Prosecutor presents each one like evidence. | Pluralizing a noun. |
| Court Reporter Quotation Marks | Quotation marks | Always a pair, opening and closing as one. Quotation marks record exactly what someone said, so the Court Reporter captures the words verbatim. | Working alone; there are always two. |
| Sheriff Semicolon | Semicolons | A western clause wrangler. A semicolon joins two closely related complete sentences without a FANBOYS word, so the Sheriff ropes them together. | Doing a comma's job. |
| Deputy Colon | Colons | An eager, organized deputy. A colon introduces what follows: a list, an explanation, or a quotation. The Deputy loves announcing it. | Introducing nothing. |
| Defense Attorney Parentheses | Parentheses | Shy and sweet; everything is an aside. Parentheses hold extra information the sentence could do without. | The main argument. |
| Assistant Defense Attorney Brackets | Brackets | Almost silent. Brackets step in only to clarify words inside a quotation. | Chatty. |
| Hyphen | Hyphens | An enthusiastic puppy who loves connecting things. A hyphen joins words into one, as in a compound describing word before a noun ("a well-known author"). | A dash. |
| Dash | Dashes | A dignified, full-grown police dog. A dash adds emphasis or interrupts a sentence. | A hyphen. |
| The Mayor | Capitalization, the Sentence Factory, the Writing Company, Wonderful Words, mixed review, and certifying | See "The Mayor" above. | Sputtering in teaching material; naming any real person. |
| Slang | Register (formal and informal language) | See "Slang" above. | Teaching a rule or certifying work. |

## Canon terms

Keep these exactly as written, and use each at most once in a piece.

- **FANBOYS**, always in capitals.
- **"Could we be more specific?"** Jake's question, always with "we."
- **"Lights! Camera!! Action Verb!!!"** Vinny's battle cry, with its one, two, and three exclamation marks.
- **Li'l Pete**, always the full name with the apostrophe after "Li."
- **The Mayor** is always "the Mayor," with a lowercase "the" in the middle of a sentence. The Mayor is a character; never name or describe any real person behind Grammaropolis.
