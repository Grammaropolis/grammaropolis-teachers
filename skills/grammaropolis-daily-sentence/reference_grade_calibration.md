# Grade calibration

Per-grade targets for sentence length, vocabulary, structure, error load, and which error types are fair game. **A warm-up is five minutes of attention, not a test.** When in doubt, go shorter and simpler; the difficulty should live in the grammar point, not in the reading.

## At a glance

| Grade | Words per sentence | Structures that fit | Fix It errors per sentence | Sentences per day |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4 to 8 | simple sentences; a pair of adjectives; *and* joining two nouns | 1 to 2 | 1 |
| 2 | 5 to 10 | simple sentences; compound subjects and predicates; short compound sentences with *and* or *but* | 1 to 2 | 1 |
| 3 | 7 to 12 | compound sentences; simple dialogue; possessive nouns | 2 | 1 |
| 4 | 8 to 15 | compound sentences; introductory words; relative pronouns (*who, which, that*) | 2 to 3 | 1 |
| 5 | 10 to 18 | introductory phrases and clauses; series; complex sentences | 2 to 3 | 1 (Friday of a mixed week: 2) |
| 6 | 10 to 20 | complex sentences; extra information set off by commas or parentheses; pronoun case | 2 to 3 | 1 to 2 |
| 7 | 12 to 22 | phrases and clauses doing different jobs; coordinate adjectives; modifiers | 3 | 1 to 2 |
| 8 | 12 to 25 | verbals; compound-complex sentences; semicolons with transition words | 3 to 4 | 1 to 2 |

Word counts are a range, not a target. A grade 7 mentor sentence of eleven words is fine if it models the focus cleanly.

**Error counts are for mixed review.** On a focused week, Wednesday's sentence carries the focus error (once or twice) plus at most one spiral item, so a focused Wednesday may sit at the low end of the range or one below it. Say so in the key if a teacher might wonder. Count errors, not changed words, and never count the Oxford comma.

## Vocabulary

- **Grades 1-2:** everyday, decodable words. Concrete nouns a child can picture (dog, bus, lunch, rain). Short, familiar invented names.
- **Grades 3-4:** familiar words plus a few richer ones that context makes clear. Content can come from class life, animals, weather, and simple science.
- **Grades 5-6:** grade-level academic words are fine. No vocabulary the student must look up to do a grammar task.
- **Grades 7-8:** mature, interesting content (a debate club, a marine biology field trip, a community garden), still free of anything sensitive.

**The word test:** if a student could get the grammar right but stall on a word, change the word.

## Commas by grade band

Commas run through every grade, but each grade band owns different uses. Match the comma use to the band before building, and check the standards extract for the matching code.

| Grades | Comma uses that are fair |
|---|---|
| 1-2 | dates (between the day and the year); single words in a series (*red, blue, and green*); letter greetings and closings (grade 2) |
| 3 | addresses (between the city and state); dialogue; the compound-sentence comma, introduced gently; series of single words, as review |
| 4 | the comma before a coordinating conjunction in a compound sentence (the grade's main comma focus); dialogue and quotations |
| 5 | items in a series, including phrases; introductory elements of five or more words; *yes* and *no*, tag questions, and direct address |
| 6 | extra information set off by commas or parentheses (nonrestrictive elements) |
| 7 | coordinate adjectives |
| 8 | commas that mark a pause or break, reviewed alongside every earlier use |

In every band, a series item counts and announces only the required commas, never the Oxford comma, and a compound-sentence item keys the comma only when each clause is five or more words long.

## Error types that are fair at each grade (Fix It)

Only use an error type at or above the grade where it appears below, and only when the class has met it. On a focused week, the errors are the focus. On a mixed-review week, rotate among the grade's fair types.

**Grade 1**
- A missing capital letter at the start of a sentence, on *I*, or on a person's name
- A missing end mark, or a period on a sentence that is clearly a question
- A plural noun missing its *-s* ("two cat")
- A missing comma between single words in a series (required commas only), or between the day and the year in a date; keep the date at the end of the sentence, so no second comma after the year is in play

**Grade 2** (adds)
- An apostrophe missing from a common contraction (*dont, cant, isnt*)
- A capital missing from a holiday, month, day of the week, or city name
- An irregular plural or past tense formed as if regular (*mouses, runned, teached*)
- A comma missing in a letter greeting or closing

**Grade 3** (adds)
- A possessive noun missing its apostrophe (*the dogs bowl* for one dog)
- Quotation marks or the comma missing in simple dialogue
- A comma missing between the city and state in an address
- A required comma missing in a series of single words (review). Word the task "This sentence is missing two commas it needs," so a series that already shows its optional last comma doesn't confuse anyone
- Subject-verb agreement (*The birds sings.*)
- A comma missing before a coordinating conjunction in a compound sentence (introduce gently; this is grade 4's main focus; each clause five or more words)
- Comparative and superlative errors (*more taller, the most fastest*)

**Grade 4** (adds)
- Frequently confused words: *its* and *it's*; *their, there,* and *they're*; *your* and *you're*; *to, too,* and *two*
- Capitalization of titles, proper nouns, and the first word of a quotation
- A sentence fragment, when the task makes the fix clear (see the answer key checks)
- Run-on sentences and comma splices (list every acceptable fix)

**Grade 5** (adds)
- A comma missing after an introductory element of five or more words (a Grammaropolis house threshold, conservative on purpose; shorter elements may go without). The item must be an opener followed by the subject and then the verb; inverted order and long subject phrases take no comma (see the answer key checks). On a Build It day, **Also correct** accepts any opener from the same family (*When, After, Before, As soon as, Because,* and the like), since the family is open-ended
- A required comma missing in a series of phrases (never the Oxford comma)
- A comma missing after *yes* or *no*, before a tag question, or in direct address (*Marisol please pass the glue.* for *Marisol, please pass the glue.*)
- An unnecessary shift in verb tense within a sentence
- Correlative conjunction mismatches (*either* with *nor*)

**Grade 6** (adds)
- Pronoun case in compound subjects and objects (*Marisol and me went*; *between you and I*)
- A pronoun whose antecedent is genuinely unclear (only when the rewrite is obvious)
- An intensive pronoun used as a subject (*Quentin and myself went*)
- An inappropriate shift in pronoun number or person

**Grade 7** (adds)
- A comma missing between coordinate adjectives, where the *and* test is decisive (*a long, quiet walk*, but never *a small wooden box*)
- Misplaced modifiers, when the funny misreading makes the error obvious (list every acceptable fix)
- A dangling participial phrase (list every acceptable fix)

**Grade 8** (adds)
- An inappropriate shift in voice, or a shift in mood from a command to a statement (see the mood trap in the answer key checks; never a was/were item)
- A semicolon misused where a comma belongs, or a comma splice before a transition word (*however, therefore*)
- Verbal errors (a dangling gerund phrase, for example)

## Mentor sentence targets (Notice)

- Model the focus **once, cleanly.** A comma mentor sentence with four commas doing three different jobs teaches nothing on a Monday.
- The sentence should sound like something worth reading: a small surprise, a vivid detail, a bit of humor. A flat sentence makes a flat warm-up.
- At grades 1-2, the noticing question should have a concrete answer ("What letter is big? Why?"). At grades 6-8, it can be open first ("What do you notice about how this sentence is built?") before a focused follow-up.

## Use It targets (Friday)

- **Grades 1-2:** a sentence frame with one blank or one choice ("I see a ____ dog."), or "write one sentence about ____ that ends with a question mark."
- **Grades 3-5:** a topic plus the move ("Write one compound sentence about lunch. Use a comma and a FANBOYS word.").
- **Grades 6-8:** the move plus a purpose ("Write a sentence for the school newsletter that uses a semicolon to join two related ideas about the book fair.").

Success criteria: two or three, each checkable at a glance by a teacher walking the room, written as a short checklist the teacher can read aloud ("My sentence has two parts that could each stand alone."). The criteria never mark a correct form wrong: a series passes with or without the Oxford comma, and no criterion asks for an optional comma unless the task itself asked students to use one.
