# Voice card

Who the characters are lives in `reference_cast_card.md`, the cast card every Grammaropolis teacher skill shares. This file says how a warm-up prompt uses them. If a prompt needs more character than the cast card gives, write the prompt plainer.

## Who leads which warm-up

The lead character is the one who owns the day's grammar point on the cast card (the topic index names the owner for each topic). One lead per day. A second character may make a single guest appearance when the grammar genuinely involves both (Connie and Chief Comma on a compound-sentence comma day), and never more than one guest.

**Slang on a formal-or-informal day.** Slang supplies the informal version as one line, clean and school-appropriate: "a note Slang left on the Mayor's desk." The task is to rewrite it for a more formal audience, and the key says the informal version isn't bad English; it's the wrong fit for that audience. Slang never explains the rule; the Mayor does.

## How a prompt works

- **One to three sentences**, read aloud in about fifteen seconds. Shorter for grades 1-2.
- **The prompt points at the task; it never gives the answer away.**
- **Explanations are complete sentences.** A short in-character exclamation ("Fear not!" or "Case file opened.") may open a prompt, once, as a standalone line. Everything that explains or instructs is a full sentence with a subject and a verb.
- **Voice through word choice, not catchphrase spam.** One signature-style line per prompt at most, and vary it across the week.
- **Friday belongs to the Mayor when a week ends in Use It.** He certifies the class's own sentences, briefly and warmly.
- **Mixed review belongs to the Mayor.** He leads every mixed-review day; a specialist may guest for one line.
- **Match the address to the age.** At grades 1-5, a warm group address fits ("sentence builders," "team"). At grades 6-8, address students plainly ("writers," "everyone," or no address), never with a cute collective name, and offer a prompts-off version that drops the character lines entirely.
- **Li'l Pete's nature facts** are optional; if you use one, it must be simple and well established (an octopus has three hearts). If you're not certain it's true, leave it out.

## Example prompt lines

Write new lines in this register. Don't reuse these lines, or the prompts in the worked example, word for word.

- **Chief Comma (grade 4, Notice):** "Eyes front. A comma is standing in today's sentence, and it has a job. Tell me the word that comes right after it."
- **Connie (grade 3, Build It):** "These two sentences keep looking at each other. Let's invite them to become one sentence. Pick the FANBOYS word that shows how the ideas connect."
- **Roger (grade 6, Fix It):** "Step right up, folks. One of my pronouns in this sentence has lost track of its noun, and frankly, I know the feeling. Find it and fix it."
- **Benny (grade 5, Name It):** "Listen up, team. Two words in this sentence tell HOW something happened. Find them both, because I don't coach half a play."
- **Lucy (grade 4, Name It):** "Let's just sit with this sentence for a minute. One verb in it isn't doing anything at all. It simply links. Which one is it?"
- **The Mayor (Friday, Use It):** "This week you noticed it, named it, fixed it, and built it. Today you write it. I will personally certify every sentence that follows the rule."

If a teacher asks for Roger as "the blues guy," he isn't one: he's a hustler. A single prompt may carry a touch of blues-flavored lament about his missing noun, but don't offer to make him sing the blues across a week, and never describe him as a singer.

## Mechanical rules (every prompt, key, and sentence)

- **Zero em dashes and zero en dashes.** Choose the punctuation the grammar calls for: comma, period, colon, semicolon, or parentheses.
- **Oxford comma** in every list of three or more that the skill writes (house style; it's never an error when a student leaves it out).
- **Canon terms** stay exactly as the cast card writes them, including "the Mayor" (lowercase "the" mid-sentence).
- **Real grammatical terms:** "the coordinating conjunction," "the linking verb," "the direct object." Where Grammaropolis has its own friendly term, the key gives the real term first with the Grammaropolis term in parentheses ("demonstrative adjective (a pointing word)," "possessive adjective (a whose-word)"), and a grade 1-3 prompt may use the Grammaropolis term alone. Never invent any other cute noun for a grammatical part.
- **Lucy's test is the equals sign.** When a prompt or key explains a linking verb, use her framing from the cast card.
- **The character is how the rule is explained.** Every prompt line states or points at the rule through who the character is, and every warm-up still works perfectly with the prompts removed (the cast card's two tests).
