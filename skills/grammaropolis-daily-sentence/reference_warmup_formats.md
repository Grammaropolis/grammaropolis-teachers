# Warm-up formats

The five day types, the default week arc, the output shape, and one fully worked week.

## The five day types

| Day type | Students... | The sentence is... | The key gives... |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Notice** | look at a correct sentence and say what the writer did | a flawless mentor sentence that models the focus clearly, once | what to notice, stated as the rule, plus one follow-up question the teacher can ask; on a series day, a note that the last comma (before *and* or *or*) is optional |
| **Name It** | find, label, or sort (circle the adverbs; which sentence joins two complete sentences?) | one sentence, or a contrast pair where only one shows the focus | every correct label, with the count stated, why each word qualifies, and an Also correct line |
| **Fix It** | correct a flawed sentence; they're told how many errors to find | a sentence with a stated number of rule errors, and nothing else wrong | the corrected sentence, a numbered Fixes (N) list with the rule for each, and an Also correct line |
| **Build It** | combine, expand, or revise sentences using the focus | two or three short correct sentences, or one plain sentence to improve | one model answer, what makes an answer right, and an Also correct line with every other acceptable answer |
| **Use It** | write their own sentence using the focus | a frame or a topic, never a sentence to copy | success criteria as a short checklist the teacher can read aloud, plus a model sentence |

**Fix It rules.**

- Always tell students how many errors there are ("This sentence has two errors."). It turns a guessing game into a hunt, and it keeps the key honest. The key's **Fixes (N):** list must have exactly that many numbered items.
- Errors are rule violations with a clear right answer: a missing capital, a missing comma before a coordinating conjunction in a compound sentence, a wrong verb form. Never a style preference, never a debated usage, and never the Oxford comma (see the answer key checks). A series Fix It announces only the required commas.
- Count errors, not changed words. A fix that rewrites a clause (a shift in voice, say) is still one error.
- The error count per sentence comes from the grade calibration.
- **Build It on a series day:** Also correct accepts the items in any order, as long as the commas are right.
- On a focused week, at most one error on Wednesday may come from an earlier skill (the spiral item); say which one in the key.

**Name It rules.**

- State the count ("There are three adjectives in this sentence."), and say whether *a, an,* and *the* count. In Grammaropolis the articles are Jake's baby adjectives, so they are adjectives, but a task about "describing words" usually means the others. Decide, and say so in the task.
- On a series day, ask only about required commas. The last comma before *and* or *or* is optional, so it's never the answer to a Name It question.
- A contrast pair (two nearly identical sentences, one with the focus and one without) is the strongest Name It design, because the difference is the lesson. Neither sentence may already show the answer: on a comma day, leave the comma out of both.

## The default week arc (one focus, five days)

| Day | Type | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Notice | Shows the move done right. This mentor sentence is the model for Friday. |
| Tuesday | Name It | Separates the move from its look-alike (the contrast pair lives here). |
| Wednesday | Fix It | The move is missing or wrong; students repair it. Optional single spiral item. |
| Thursday | Build It | Students make the move themselves from given parts. |
| Friday | Use It | Students write their own; the Mayor certifies. |

All five days share the focus. They may share a light theme too (one class trip across the week), which makes the week feel like a story. Keep each day's sentence new; never reuse Monday's sentence later in the week.

**A second week** (six to ten days in one request) runs the same arc again on the next focus: the teacher's choice, or the natural next step from the topic index, with the first week's focus as its spiral item.

**A mixed-review week** uses Fix It every day, led by the Mayor. Each sentence mixes two or three error types the grade has met, rotating so no type appears every day, and Friday is a slightly longer two-sentence edit. If the teacher lists what the class has covered, draw only from that list.

**Teacher overrides win.** "Mentor sentences only," "just fix-its," or "four days, we have a holiday" are all fine; adjust the arc and keep the Monday-to-Friday build where it still makes sense.

## Timing

Each day runs about five minutes: roughly one minute to read the prompt and sentence, two to three minutes of student work, and a minute or two to go over the key. Friday can run seven or eight.

## Output shape

Deliver one message with two parts. The first part is safe to project; the answers are only in the second.

**The student block.** Each day's board section is one line for the teacher to read aloud, then one plain code block holding only what students see. Inside the block:

- No markdown styling (no bold, italics, or headings), no character name or label, and no answers. Anything that must be retyped is a failure.
- One sentence per line, so the teacher can paste it onto a slide in large type. Contrast pairs are labeled "A." and "B." at the start of their lines.
- Target words are marked in plain text: in quotation marks, or in capitals with the task saying "the word in capitals."
- A blank line, then the task.

**Easier and Stretch.** Any day may carry one optional **Easier** version and one optional **Stretch** version in the key. Each is formatted exactly like a student block (a plain code block with the sentence on its own line, a blank line, then the task), followed by its own answer outside the block. They pass every answer key check, just like the main item. Leave them out when the day doesn't need them.

**Prompts-off version.** Every day works with the read-aloud line removed. At grades 6-8, offer this version in the closing line; build it on request by dropping the read-aloud lines and keeping everything else.

**Printable week strip (on request).** All five student blocks in one plain code block, each day introduced by a single line such as "MONDAY" and separated by a blank line, sized to print on one page. Answers stay in the key.

## Worked example: grade 4, commas in compound sentences, a week of five

Every sentence here was checked against the answer key checks. Every compound sentence has five or more words in each clause, so its comma is required rather than optional. Use it as the model for shape, length, and tone. Don't reuse these sentences or prompts.

# Grade 4 daily sentences: commas in compound sentences (week of five)

## For the board

### Monday: Notice
Read aloud (Chief Comma): "Listen up. One comma is on duty in today's sentence. Study the words on both sides of it, and tell me why it's posted there."

```
The storm knocked out the power, so we ate dinner by flashlight.

What word comes right after the comma?
Cover up everything after the comma. Is the first part a complete sentence on its own?
```

### Tuesday: Name It
Read aloud (Connie): "I love a good connection, and both of these sentences have one. Only one of them joins two complete sentences, though. Can you find it?"

```
A. Marisol grabbed her striped umbrella and ran to the bus stop.
B. Marisol grabbed her striped umbrella and Quentin grabbed his yellow raincoat.

Which sentence joins two complete sentences? Where should its comma go?
```

### Wednesday: Fix It
Read aloud (Chief Comma): "Two mistakes got past my desk overnight. That won't happen again. Find them both."

```
Our class planted tomato seeds on monday but only three of them have sprouted.

This sentence has two errors. Find them and fix them.
```

### Thursday: Build It
Read aloud (Connie): "These two sentences clearly know each other. Let's introduce them properly, with one comma and one FANBOYS word."

```
The school library closed early today.
We read our books on the front steps.

Join them into one sentence using a comma and a FANBOYS word (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so).
```

### Friday: Use It
Read aloud (the Mayor): "Sentence builders, you've spent four days studying this move. Today you make one of your own. Every sentence that follows the rule earns my certification."

```
Write one compound sentence about your weekend plans.
Use a comma and a FANBOYS word to join two complete sentences.
```

## Teacher key

### Monday: Notice
- Answer: The word after the comma is *so*, a coordinating conjunction. Yes: "The storm knocked out the power" is a complete sentence, and so is "we ate dinner by flashlight."
- Why: When a coordinating conjunction joins two complete sentences, a comma goes before the conjunction.
- Follow-up question: Could we swap *so* for *and*? (Yes, and the sentence still works, but *so* tells us the storm caused the flashlight dinner.)
- Standard: L.4.2.C (comma before a coordinating conjunction in a compound sentence)

### Tuesday: Name It
- Answer: Sentence B. Its comma goes right before *and*: "Marisol grabbed her striped umbrella, and Quentin grabbed his yellow raincoat."
- Why: B has two subjects doing two things (Marisol grabbed, Quentin grabbed), so it's two complete sentences joined by *and*; A has one subject, Marisol, doing two things, so it takes no comma.
- Also correct: none.
- Watch for: Students who put a comma before every *and*. Sentence A is the reason not to.
- Standard: L.4.2.C (comma before a coordinating conjunction in a compound sentence)

### Wednesday: Fix It
- Corrected: Our class planted tomato seeds on Monday, but only three of them have sprouted.
- Fixes (2):
  1. Add a comma before *but*: *but* joins two complete sentences ("Our class planted tomato seeds on Monday" and "only three of them have sprouted").
  2. Capitalize *Monday*: the days of the week are proper nouns. (This is the week's spiral item.)
- Also correct: Two sentences instead of one: "Our class planted tomato seeds on Monday. But only three of them have sprouted." Starting a sentence with *But* is correct.
- Easier:
  ```
  Our class planted tomato seeds on Monday but only three of them have sprouted.

  This sentence has one error. Find it and fix it.
  ```
  Answer: Our class planted tomato seeds on Monday, but only three of them have sprouted. Fixes (1): add a comma before *but*. Also correct: two sentences ("...on Monday. But only three of them have sprouted.").
- Stretch:
  ```
  My brother fed the goldfish on tuesday but he didnt feed the hamster.

  This sentence has three errors. Find them and fix them.
  ```
  Answer: My brother fed the goldfish on Tuesday, but he didn't feed the hamster. Fixes (3): 1. capitalize *Tuesday* (a day of the week); 2. add a comma before *but* (it joins two complete sentences); 3. add the apostrophe in *didn't* (it marks the missing *o* in *did not*). Also correct: two sentences ("...on Tuesday. But he didn't feed the hamster.").
- Standard: L.4.2.C (comma before a coordinating conjunction in a compound sentence); spiral: L.4.2.A (correct capitalization)

### Thursday: Build It
- Model: The school library closed early today, so we read our books on the front steps.
- Why: A comma plus a coordinating conjunction joins two complete sentences; *so* shows that the closed library caused the reading outside.
- Also correct: "The school library closed early today, but we read our books on the front steps." (*But* or *yet* shows the class read anyway.) "We read our books on the front steps, for the school library closed early today." (The order flips because *for* gives the reason; it sounds formal, but it's correct.) "The school library closed early today, and we read our books on the front steps" is correct too, though it hides how the two ideas connect; it's a good one to discuss.
- Standard: L.4.2.C (comma before a coordinating conjunction in a compound sentence)

### Friday: Use It
- Success criteria (read these aloud):
  - [ ] My sentence has two parts that could each stand alone, each with its own subject and verb.
  - [ ] I put a comma right before my FANBOYS word.
  - [ ] My FANBOYS word does the joining.
- Model: My cousin is visiting on Saturday, and we're going to build a birdhouse.
- Watch for: Sentences with one subject and two verbs ("I will swim and eat pizza"). They are correct sentences, but they're not compound, and they take no comma.
- Easier:
  ```
  I want to play outside after school, but ____.

  Finish the sentence with a complete sentence of your own.
  ```
  Answer: any complete sentence with its own subject and verb, such as "it might rain."
- Stretch:
  ```
  Write two compound sentences about your weekend.
  Use a different FANBOYS word in each one.
  ```
  Answer: each sentence meets all three criteria.
- Standard: L.4.2.C (comma before a coordinating conjunction in a compound sentence)

For next week's request: "Last week: commas in compound sentences. Spiral in: [one error type]."
