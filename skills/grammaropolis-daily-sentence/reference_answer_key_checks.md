# Answer key checks

Grammar is the whole point of Grammaropolis. A teacher who projects a wrong answer key in front of thirty students stops trusting everything else this skill makes. Read the "Never mark these as errors" list and the topic traps below **before drafting**, then run every check on every item, in order, before delivering. They take a moment each, and they are the reason a teacher can use the key without checking it.

## The eight checks

### 1. Solve it cold

Cover your own key. Read only what students will see (the sentence and the task) and do the task from scratch, the way a careful student at that grade would. Name the rule that decides each answer ("a comma goes before a coordinating conjunction that joins two complete sentences"). Then compare with the key.

- If they disagree, the item is wrong. Fix the sentence or the task, not the explanation.
- If you can't name a rule, the item is testing a preference. Replace it.

### 2. Hunt for a second right answer

List every answer a careful student could defend, not just the one you meant.

- **More than one defensible answer, and they all teach the same rule:** list the others under **Also correct**.
- **More than one, and they teach different rules, or the "right" one depends on what the writer meant:** tighten the task ("Add one comma," "Fix it by adding one word") or rewrite the sentence.
- **Fix It items with several standard repairs** (run-ons, comma splices, fragments, dangling modifiers) always list every standard repair in the key. A run-on, for example, can be fixed with a period and a capital, with a comma and a coordinating conjunction, with a semicolon (grade 5 and up), with a semicolon plus a transition word and a comma ("; however,"), or by making one clause dependent. When the second clause explains the first, a colon works too.
- **Every Fix It, Name It, and Build It key shows an Also correct line**, even when the answer is "none." Writing "none" is a promise that you looked.

### 3. Count every fix-it

Write the corrected sentence, then compare it with the flawed sentence and list each error once, in a numbered **Fixes (N):** list.

- **Count errors, not changed words.** One error can take several words to fix: a shift in voice or mood may rewrite a whole clause, and a run-on split in two changes a mark and a capital. That is one fix.
- N must equal the number of errors you told students.
- Every difference between the two versions must belong to one of the numbered fixes. The only permitted unnumbered difference is the Oxford comma in a series, which the corrected sentence shows as house style and the key marks "(house style, not counted)."
- Nothing else in the flawed sentence may be wrong. The classic accident: a second, unintended error (a lowercase proper noun, a misspelled word) that a sharp student finds and the key doesn't mention. Outside a series-comma item, write any series in the flawed sentence with its Oxford comma already in place, so the comparison stays clean.
- The corrected sentence must itself pass every other check. Correcting a sentence into a new error is the worst possible key.

### 4. Check every label

For Name It tasks, confirm the job of every word in THIS sentence, not the job it usually does.

- State the count in the task or the key, and make sure the count is right.
- State whether *a, an,* and *the* count as adjectives. In Grammaropolis they are adjectives (Jake's baby adjectives); if the task wants only the others, say "not counting *a, an,* and *the*."
- Watch the shape-shifters (table below). If a word in the sentence could plausibly be labeled two ways, either it's the point of the task, or it comes out of the sentence.

### 5. Check the mentor sentence is flawless

A Notice sentence must be correct in every respect, not just the focus: spelling, punctuation, agreement, and capitalization, with the Oxford comma in any series as house style. It should model the focus once and clearly, and read naturally aloud.

### 6. Check the content

- Every factual claim is true and well established, or the sentence makes no factual claim.
- No real people, brands, companies, real schools, or real students. Invented names from a mix of backgrounds.
- Nothing frightening, political, mean, or embarrassing. Every sentence passes the back-cover test: fine printed on the back of a book in front of any teacher or parent.
- No student data anywhere.

### 7. Check the mechanics of the whole delivery

- **Zero em dashes and zero en dashes** in sentences, prompts, keys, and the surrounding reply. Search the draft for them before delivering. Number ranges use "to."
- US spelling and punctuation: periods and commas inside closing quotation marks.
- The Oxford comma in every corrected, model, or mentor sentence with a series of three or more, and the no-Oxford version under **Also correct** whenever a key's answer contains a series.
- Grade calibration holds: word counts, vocabulary, and error load.
- Each day's student block contains no answers, no markdown styling, and no character labels, with one sentence per line.
- Easier and Stretch lines, when present, carry their own answers and pass these same checks.

### 8. Check the voice

- Each prompt matches the character's identity on the cast card (Roger is a charming hustler, Benny is a coach, Lucy is a philosopher, Connie is a connector, Vinny is a superhero, the Mayor is the warm certifier).
- Explanations are complete sentences; at most one short in-character exclamation per prompt.
- Real grammatical terms in the key, with the Grammaropolis term in parentheses where one exists; FANBOYS in capitals.
- The prompt doesn't give away the answer.

## Never mark these as errors

These are debated, optional, or simply correct. They make bad Fix It items because a well-read student, parent, or colleague can rightly object. Never key them as errors, never make one the missing piece a student must supply, and never mark a student wrong for using either form.

| Usage | Why it's not a Fix It error |
|---|---|
| The Oxford comma, present or absent | Style guides differ. Model it in every correct sentence as Grammaropolis house style, but never count it as an error, and always list the no-Oxford version under **Also correct**. A series Fix It announces only the required commas (in "apples grapes and pears," only the comma after *apples*). |
| No comma after an introductory element of four or fewer words (*In the morning we left.* or *When it rained we stayed in.*) | Major style guides allow leaving it out after a short introductory element. Key an introductory comma only when the element is **five or more words** long and is an opener followed by the subject and then the verb (see the introductory-elements trap). Five words is a Grammaropolis house threshold, set on the conservative side on purpose, since the guides say only "short." |
| No comma between two short independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction (*I ran and she walked.*) | Optional when the clauses are short. Key the comma before the conjunction only when **each** clause is five or more words long (the same house threshold). |
| No comma before *so* when it means "so that" (*We left early so we could get seats.*) | Here *so* introduces a purpose, not a second complete sentence, and it takes no comma. Never key a missing comma before a *so* that means "so that." Build compound-sentence items with *so* meaning "as a result" ("It rained all morning at the park, so the soccer game was canceled."). |
| Starting a sentence with *And, But,* or *Because* | Correct when the sentence is complete. ("Because it rained" alone is a fragment; that's a different error.) |
| Ending a sentence with a preposition | Standard English. |
| Splitting an infinitive (*to boldly go*) | Standard English. |
| Singular *they* for a person whose gender is unknown or not stated | Accepted by major US style guides. |
| *That* versus *which* in a clause that limits the noun (*the bike that I fixed*, *the bike which I fixed*) | Both are standard. Only the comma question around a nonrestrictive clause is keyable, and only when the sentence settles the meaning. |
| *That* versus *who* for people (*the girl that won*) | Both are standard. |
| *Who* versus *whom* as an object (*the teacher who we thanked*) | Too contested for any warm-up, even in writing. |
| *Who* versus *which* or *that* for an animal (*the puppy who wagged its tail*) | Writers use both, especially for pets. A *who* versus *which* item contrasts only a person with an object. |
| "I feel good," "The soup smells good" | A linking verb plus an adjective is standard. *Good* after a linking verb describes the subject. Key *good* versus *well* only after a clear action verb ("She sang well"). |
| A pronoun after *than* or *as* (*taller than me*, *as tall as I*) | Both forms appear in edited English. |
| A pronoun after a linking verb (*It's me*, *It is I*) | Both are standard; "It's me" is the everyday form. |
| A pronoun or noun before a gerund (*him helping*, *his helping*) | Both appear in edited English. |
| *If I was* versus *if I were*, and *I wish I was* versus *I wish I were* | *Was* is common in standard informal writing. Never build a was/were item. |
| The mood of a real conditional (*If it rains, we'll stay inside.*) | Sources label it differently. Never ask students to name it. |
| *Less* versus *fewer* | Usage guides disagree about where the line falls ("less than ten minutes," "one less thing"). |
| Notional agreement: collective nouns and "a group of" phrases (*The team is* or *are*, *A group of kids is* or *are*) | Writers go both ways, especially when the members act separately. Use collective nouns only in sentences where the singular reading is the only natural one (*The team is ready.*), and never key the verb. |
| *None is* versus *none are* | Both are standard. |
| *There is* or *there are* before a compound (*There is a pencil and two erasers.*) | Both appear in edited English. Only a single noun after the verb is keyable ("There are three erasers on the desk."). |
| A hyphen in a compound after a linking verb (*The author is well known* or *well-known*) | Dictionaries differ. Key a compound-modifier hyphen only before the noun (*a well-known author*). |
| Apostrophes in plurals of letters (*mind your p's and q's*, *two a's*) | The one standard exception to "plurals never take apostrophes." Avoid letter plurals in apostrophe items. |
| Passive voice | A choice, not an error, unless a sentence shifts voice awkwardly at grade 8. |
| Contractions | Correct. Only a register question on formal-or-informal days. |
| *Hopefully* meaning "I hope" | Accepted in modern usage. |
| Possessives of singular names ending in *s* (*James's, James'*) | Style guides differ. Avoid these names in apostrophe items. |
| Commas before *too* or after *also* | Optional. |
| Number style (*seven* or *7*) | A style choice. |

## Shape-shifters: words whose part of speech depends on the sentence

Check these every time they appear in a Name It sentence.

| Word(s) | Can be |
|---|---|
| *well* | adverb (She sang well), adjective meaning healthy (I feel well), interjection (Well, maybe) |
| *fast, hard, late, early, straight* | adjective or adverb, with no *-ly* |
| *home, outside, inside, upstairs* | noun, adverb, or adjective |
| *after, before, since, until* | preposition (after lunch) or subordinating conjunction (after we ate) |
| *up, down, in, out, off, over* | preposition (up the hill) or adverb, including in phrasal verbs (She looked up; they gave in) |
| *that, this, these, those* | demonstrative pronoun when it stands alone (That is mine) or demonstrative adjective (a pointing word) before a noun (that book). In Grammaropolis the second kind belongs to Jake's family. In the key, write the real term first with the Grammaropolis term in parentheses; a grade 1-3 prompt may say "pointing word" alone. *That* can also introduce a clause (I know that it's late; the book that I read), so keep it out of pronoun and adjective counts unless it's the point |
| *my, your, his, her, its, our, their* | Before a noun (her book), these are possessive adjectives (whose-words), which Grammaropolis places in Jake's family; the Common Core itself calls them possessive pronouns (L.1.1.D). In the key, write "possessive adjective (a whose-word), also called a possessive pronoun"; a grade 1-3 prompt may say "whose-word" alone. **Always accept both labels, possessive pronoun and possessive adjective, and never mark either wrong.** *Her* can also be an object pronoun (I saw her), and *his* can stand alone as a pronoun (The book is his). Avoid asking students to label them unless the task is about them |
| Linking or action: *feel, look, smell, taste, sound, appear, grow, turn, stay, remain* | Use Lucy the Linking Verb's equals-sign test. A linking verb links the subject to information that renames or describes the subject, so swap the verb for an equals sign: if the subject really equals the word after the verb, the verb is linking. "The soup smells good" (soup = good: linking); "The dog smells the soup" (dog = soup? no: action). "The leaves turned orange" (leaves = orange: linking); "Rosalind turned the key" (action). *Appear, stay,* and *remain* can also be action verbs with no complement at all ("A rabbit appeared"; "Three cookies remain"). |
| Linking in the sentences you write: *seem, become* (and forms of *be* before a noun or adjective) | Write *seem* and *become* only in their linking sense in these items. (*Become* has a rare action sense, "That hat becomes you," so keep it out.) "She became a doctor" passes the equals-sign test (she = a doctor), even though swapping in *is* would change the meaning, which is why the key never uses a swap-in-*is* test. Forms of *be* are linking when a noun or adjective after them renames or describes the subject ("Quentin is tall") and helping verbs before another verb ("Quentin is running"). **Keep two *be* patterns out of linking-verb items entirely:** *be* followed by a place or time phrase ("The cat is under the bed," "The party is at noon"), where the equals-sign test fails, and *there is* or *there are* |
| Nouns used as describers (*school bus, apple pie*) | The first word is a noun doing an adjective's job; say which answer you accept, or avoid them in adjective and noun counts |
| *-ing* words | verb (She is running), gerund (Running is fun), or participle (the running water). At grades 4-8, a gerund or participle is Vinny in costume. After a linking *is*, an *-ing* word may be a gerund ("Her hobby is painting") or an adjective ("The movie is boring"), so the sentence must pin the role |
| *but, for, so, yet* | coordinating conjunctions when they join; but *for* can be a preposition (for the team), *so* and *yet* can be adverbs (so happy, not yet), *so* can mean "so that" (see the never-mark list), and *but* can be a preposition (everyone but me) |
| *like* | preposition (like a rocket), verb (I like it), or casual filler |
| *there* | adverb (over there) or the opener of a sentence whose subject comes after the verb (There are three cats: the subject is *cats*) |

## Common traps in each topic

- **Commas in compound sentences:** a compound predicate (one subject, two verbs) takes no comma. Build Fix It sentences with two real subjects and at least five words in each clause, so the comma is required rather than optional. Watch for *so* meaning "so that," which takes no comma.
- **Commas in a series:** only the last comma (the one before *and* or *or*) is optional; every other comma between items is required. On every series day, count and announce only the required commas: a Fix It says "This sentence is missing two commas it needs," and a Name It asks only about required commas. The corrected sentence shows the Oxford comma as house style, not counted, and **Also correct** gives the version without it. On a Notice day, the key says that the last comma is optional. On a Build It day, **Also correct** accepts the items in any order.
- **Introductory elements:** an introductory element is an opener (a phrase or a dependent clause) that comes **before the subject**. Pin every intro-comma item to the pattern opener, then subject, then verb ("After the long bus ride to the museum, the class ate lunch."), and key the comma only when the opener is five or more words. Two look-alikes take no comma at all, however long they are: inverted order, where the verb comes before the subject ("Down the long hall to the office walked Tomas."), and a long subject phrase ("Running laps after school every day is hard."), where the whole phrase IS the subject and a comma would split it from its verb. *Yes* and *no* at the start of a sentence, a tag question, and direct address are separate rules (grade 5), and they are always keyable.
- **Fragments:** "Because the bus was late." is a fragment; "The bus was late." is not. Make sure every "complete" sentence you present really is complete, and that commands ("Close the door.") are recognized as complete, with *you* as the understood subject.
- **Run-ons and comma splices:** list every standard repair (see check 2), unless the task names the method.
- **Subjects:** in questions, commands, and *there is* sentences, the subject is often not the first word. Say so in the key.
- **Subject-verb agreement:** the verb agrees with the subject, not the nearest noun ("The box of crayons sits"). Two subjects joined by *and* take a plural verb. With *or* or *nor*, the verb agrees with the closer subject. *Everyone, somebody,* and *nobody* are singular. Offer choices in one tense only, or pin the tense with a time word, because a past-tense verb agrees with any subject.
- **Pronoun case:** test it with compound subjects ("Marisol and I went"), compound objects ("between you and me"), and objects of prepositions. Never test it after *than* or *as*, after a linking verb, or before a gerund.
- **Pronoun-antecedent:** avoid sentences where *he* or *she* could honestly refer to either of two people unless unclear reference is the lesson.
- **Relative pronouns:** a *who* versus *which* item contrasts a person with an object ("the pilot who landed the plane," "the kite which crashed"). Never key *that* versus *which*, *who* versus *that*, *who* versus *whom*, or anything about animals.
- **Restrictive and nonrestrictive clauses:** "My sister who lives in Ohio" and "My sister, who lives in Ohio," are both correct and mean different things. Only use these when the sentence makes the meaning unmistakable (for example, "My only sister, who...").
- **Appositives:** the same rule covers a name or noun phrase that renames a noun. "My partner Rosalind" and "My partner, Rosalind," are both correct and mean different things (one partner among several, or the only one). Never key the commas unless the sentence pins the meaning, as a name after "my only sister" does.
- **Verb mood (grade 8):** a mood-shift item fixes a sentence that starts as a command and turns into a statement ("Clean your desk, and then you should stack the books." becomes "Clean your desk, and then stack the books."). Never key was/were, the mood of a real conditional, or a sentence with *should, would,* or *could* as a mood label.
- **Capitalization:** seasons are lowercase; *mom* is capitalized as a name (I asked Mom) but not after *my* (my mom); directions are lowercase unless they name a region.
- **Quotation marks:** US style puts periods and commas inside the closing mark; question marks go inside only when the quoted words are the question.
- **Apostrophes:** *its* (possessive) never has one; plural nouns ending in *s* take the apostrophe after the *s* (the girls' coats); plural nouns never take apostrophes (*apple's for sale* is the error, not the fix), with one exception: plurals of letters (*p's and q's*), which you should keep out of apostrophe items.
- **Semicolons:** a period and a semicolon are both correct between two closely related sentences, so never ask students to choose between them; a semicolon needs a complete sentence on each side.
- **Hyphens:** a compound modifier before a noun takes a hyphen (*a well-known author*); after a linking verb it varies, so never key it; an *-ly* adverb never takes one (*a quickly moving car*).
- **Good and well:** after a linking verb, *good* is correct ("I feel good"); key *well* only after an action verb ("The team played well").
- **Titles of books and movies** need italics or underlining, which a projected slide may not show. Avoid title-formatting errors unless the teacher asks, and then tell them to underline.
